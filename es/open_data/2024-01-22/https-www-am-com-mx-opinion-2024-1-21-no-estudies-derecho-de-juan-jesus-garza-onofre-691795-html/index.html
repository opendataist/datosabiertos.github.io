Guanajuato faces a critical oversaturation of legal professionals, evidenced by thousands of new graduates and a significant mismatch between training and actual employment needs. This disparity highlights broader inefficiencies in the state’s workforce planning, where legal fields are flooded while essential sectors like medicine and engineering remain critically understaffed. The data reveals that many lawyers do not practice their discipline, underscoring a systemic disconnect between higher education outputs and real economic demands. The root of this crisis lies in the unchecked proliferation of law schools without adequate quality supervision, leading to a devaluation of the profession. Expert criticism emphasizes that this lack of regulation prioritizes profit over social responsibility and legal ethics. Consequently, the field struggles with poor professional standards and complex, obscure language, which hinders transparency and public access to justice. The situation reflects a deteriorating professional culture that fails to adapt to societal needs. This article is relevant to open_data because it demonstrates how releasing government statistics can expose urgent structural problems in education and labor markets. By making enrollment and employment figures public, citizens and policymakers can scrutinize the quality and relevance of academic programs. Such transparency is essential for driving accountability, encouraging regulatory reform, and aligning educational offerings with the genuine needs of society, thereby fostering a more effective and just legal system.
