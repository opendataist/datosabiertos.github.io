The article highlights a critical gap between the pervasive sharing of personal data on social media and the resulting security vulnerabilities in Latin America. It reveals that extensive daily engagement and the voluntary disclosure of sensitive information, such as geolocation and personal photos, have made users highly susceptible to fraud. This lack of digital hygiene has led to a significant portion of the population becoming victims of scams, underscoring the urgent need for greater awareness regarding the permanence and accessibility of online footprints. Consequently, the main conclusion emphasizes that users must actively manage their digital privacy settings to mitigate these risks. Rather than passively accepting default configurations, individuals are urged to regularly audit their privacy controls, revoke unnecessary third-party access, and employ robust authentication methods like complex passwords and two-factor authentication. This shift from passive usage to active management is presented as the essential defense against identity theft and unauthorized data exploitation. This content is relevant to open data because it illustrates the dual nature of data transparency: while open data initiatives aim to improve societal access to information, individual exposure on social platforms creates unregulated data flows that compromise personal security. It underscores the importance of balancing openness with privacy rights, demonstrating how the "open" sharing of personal details by users can lead to tangible harms like fraud. Thus, it calls for better data governance and user education to protect individuals in an era where personal information is increasingly commodified and exposed.
