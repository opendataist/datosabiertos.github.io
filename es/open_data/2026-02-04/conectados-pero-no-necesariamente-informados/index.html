Conectados, pero no necesariamente informados

The article argues that expanding internet access does not automatically produce a more informed electorate, challenging the common assumption that digital connectivity equates to better political knowledge. While increased access fosters a more competitive and less predictable voting landscape by lowering campaign costs and weakening traditional intermediaries, it simultaneously amplifies emotional engagement and tribalism. This suggests that the primary shift is in the intensity of political competition rather than the rationality of voter decision-making. Data analysis reveals that while internet connectivity reduces voter loyalty and makes elections closer, it often strengthens partisan hostility through echo chambers and targeted messaging. Empirical evidence indicates that social media usage yields minimal gains in factual political knowledge and correlates with greater susceptibility to unverified claims. Consequently, the volume and speed of information dissemination tend to prioritize emotional resonance over deliberative understanding, leading to polarization rather than enlightenment. This insight is crucial for open data initiatives, as it highlights that providing raw data is insufficient without contextual literacy. The relevance lies in the need for robust media literacy and easy-to-verify public information to complement technological access. To truly enhance democratic engagement, societies must prioritize educational frameworks that enable citizens to critically assess information, ensuring that increased connectivity supports factual comprehension and social cohesion rather than mere fragmentation and distrust.
