The article highlights the critical intersection between indigenous political-electoral rights and electoral justice in Mexico, emphasizing the significant demographic presence of indigenous peoples in the nation’s political landscape. It argues that recognizing and protecting these rights is essential for strengthening democratic inclusivity and addressing historical marginalization, particularly for indigenous women who face double discrimination based on gender and ethnicity. Judicial rulings demonstrate substantial progress in establishing legal precedents that protect vulnerable sectors. Electoral tribunals have increasingly adopted intercultural perspectives and reversed the burden of proof, requiring officials to prove they did not commit gender-based political violence. These legal mechanisms ensure that indigenous women can exercise their public offices fully and without hindrance, transforming abstract human rights principles into actionable legal protections. This topic is highly relevant to open data because transparent, accessible, and disaggregated data is the foundation for monitoring these judicial trends and measuring the real impact of electoral reforms. Open data initiatives must go beyond basic demographic statistics to track specific indicators of violence, access to office, and judicial outcomes. By making this specialized information publicly available, stakeholders can better analyze systemic barriers and advocate for policies that guarantee equal political participation for indigenous communities.
