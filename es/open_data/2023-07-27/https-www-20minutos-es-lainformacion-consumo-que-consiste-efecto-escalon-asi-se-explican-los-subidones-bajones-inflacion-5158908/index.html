The article concludes that while Spain’s inflation rate cooled to 1.9% in June 2023, this does not indicate a decrease in the actual cost of living. The apparent relief is largely due to the "stepping effect," where current comparisons are made against the exceptionally high inflation rates of the previous year. Consequently, consumers are paying less than they would have under the previous surge, but prices continue to rise cumulatively. Essentially, goods and services are still becoming more expensive, just at a slower pace, meaning the purchasing power of consumers has not increased in absolute terms. This phenomenon highlights a critical distinction between inflation rates and price levels, which is highly relevant to open data initiatives. When public institutions publish statistical indices like the Consumer Price Index (IPC), the raw numerical decrease can be easily misinterpreted by the media and the general public as a return to affordability. Without proper context or transparent visualization of cumulative price levels, users of open data might erroneously believe that economic hardship has ended, leading to misguided public discourse and policy evaluations. Therefore, the real-world implication is that while the acceleration of inflation has moderated, the absolute cost of daily necessities remains higher than it was a year ago. For open data advocates, this case study underscores the necessity of not just publishing raw numbers, but also providing explanatory metadata and intuitive data visualizations. It is crucial to help non-expert users distinguish between the *rate of change* and the *absolute value*, ensuring that statistical transparency leads to accurate societal understanding rather than superficial optimism.
