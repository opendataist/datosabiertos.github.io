Me llamo Salvador

The article highlights how the name "Salvador" transcends mere nomenclature to become a powerful vessel for political memory and resistance in Chile. By documenting the stories of individuals named Salvador across five decades, it reveals how this choice serves as a persistent act of defiance against the dictatorship and a continuous homage to Salvador Allende. The narrative demonstrates that naming is not a passive tradition but an active, sometimes dangerous, political gesture that embeds historical consciousness into the identity of each new generation. The central conclusion is that the legacy of Allende remains alive and relevant through these personal testimonies, creating an unbroken chain of moral and political dignity. From those named before the coup to parents in modern democracy, the name functions as a "talisman of life" amidst historical trauma. It challenges negationist discourses by physically embodying the values of justice and democratic defense, proving that the figure of Allende cannot be erased by time or authoritarian repression. This piece is highly relevant to open data because it contextualizes quantitative information, such as civil registry statistics obtained through transparency laws, with qualitative human narratives. It illustrates how open datasets can uncover hidden social patterns and collective memories when analyzed alongside personal accounts. Furthermore, it underscores the importance of understanding the socio-political implications behind raw data, showing that statistics on names are actually records of civic resistance and historical identity preservation.
