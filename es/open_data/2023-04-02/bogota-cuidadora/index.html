Bogotá ¿cuidadora?

The article critiques a recent Bogotá initiative requiring citizens to register personal data to access public spaces, drawing sharp parallels to a failed historical proposal by a past mayor. That earlier attempt, which demanded detailed inventories and routines to prevent theft, was rejected due to public fear of police corruption and data misuse. The current digital version, despite its benevolent name, triggers similar alarms regarding surveillance overreach and the potential for state control, echoing authoritarian precedents. This recurring pattern highlights a fundamental tension between public security narratives and individual privacy rights. The government’s insistence on collecting excessive personal information under the guise of protection suggests a disregard for civil liberties and trust in public institutions. The public’s consistent resistance to such measures underscores the importance of data sovereignty and the right to refuse intrusive monitoring, even when presented as necessary for health or safety. This case is highly relevant to open data principles because it illustrates the critical necessity of transparency, consent, and data minimization in civic technology. It serves as a cautionary tale for developers and policymakers: without robust privacy safeguards and genuine openness regarding data usage, digital initiatives risk becoming tools of surveillance rather than empowerment. True open data must prioritize user agency and protect citizens from authoritarian data collection practices, ensuring technology serves the public good rather than state control.
