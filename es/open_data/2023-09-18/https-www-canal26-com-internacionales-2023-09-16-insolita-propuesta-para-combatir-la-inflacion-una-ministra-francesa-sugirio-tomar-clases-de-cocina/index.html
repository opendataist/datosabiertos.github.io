The article highlights a controversial proposal by a French minister to reintroduce cooking classes in schools as a strategy to combat high inflation, specifically targeting the rising cost of processed foods. The underlying assumption is that teaching consumers to prepare raw ingredients from scratch will reduce reliance on expensive pre-made meals, thereby lowering household food expenditures. This suggestion frames the economic crisis as a deficit in domestic culinary skills rather than a systemic issue of purchasing power. However, this approach has sparked significant criticism for demonstrating a disconnect from the realities of low-income households. Political opponents argue that the proposal reveals a dismissive attitude toward social justice, emphasizing that hunger stems from a lack of resources rather than a lack of knowledge. The debate underscores the tension between policy solutions that target consumer behavior and those that address the fundamental inability of families to afford basic necessities. This discussion is highly relevant to open data because the evidence challenging the minister’s stance comes from transparent surveys conducted by COP1 and IFOP. These studies reveal that nearly half of students are skipping meals due to price hikes, providing verifiable data that contradicts the idea that culinary education is the primary solution. The availability and analysis of such open statistical data are crucial for holding policymakers accountable and ensuring that responses to inflation are grounded in factual evidence regarding household deprivation rather than ideological assumptions.
