La soledad creciente de España: los mayores de 65 que viven solos se disparan más de un 20% en la última década

The latest census data reveals a profound structural shift in Spanish society: the normalization of living alone. With one in four households now consisting of a single person, surpassing those with two members, there is a clear trend toward individualization. This evolution is not limited to younger demographics but is particularly driven by an aging population, where the number of seniors living independently has surged significantly over the last decade. This phenomenon highlights changing social dynamics and demographic realities that transcend traditional family models. The gender dimension of this trend is stark, as women over sixty-five constitute the vast majority of single-person households. This disparity reflects longer life expectancies and societal changes affecting older adults. Furthermore, the geographic distribution of this phenomenon contrasts coastal resort towns and aging inland cities, which exhibit high rates of solitary living, against certain Madrid suburbs where multi-person households remain more common. These patterns suggest that both lifestyle choices and demographic pressures, such as urbanization and migration, heavily influence housing arrangements across different regions. This article is highly relevant to open data initiatives because it illustrates how public statistics can uncover critical socio-economic patterns invisible to casual observation. By analyzing census data alongside housing tenure metrics—where ownership remains dominant but rental markets are gaining ground—researchers can better understand the pressure on social services and housing policies. Open access to such granular data enables policymakers to address the needs of an isolated, aging population and adapt urban planning to reflect the reality of smaller, more numerous households.
