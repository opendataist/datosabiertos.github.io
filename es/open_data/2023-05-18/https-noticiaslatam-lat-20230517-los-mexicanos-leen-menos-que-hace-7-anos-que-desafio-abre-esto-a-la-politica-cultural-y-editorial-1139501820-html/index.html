The article highlights a significant decline in reading habits in Mexico over the past seven years, revealing that fewer citizens engage with textual materials. This trend underscores a historical lack of systematic state policy for literacy promotion, where efforts have remained fragmented rather than integrated. The data suggests that without targeted interventions, the cultural gap continues to widen, particularly affecting marginalized groups and those without access to diverse literary resources beyond mandatory school texts. Furthermore, the discussion exposes structural issues within the publishing market and public policy. Critics argue that government initiatives have failed to diversify literary canon or effectively support independent publishers, leading to a dominance by large multinational conglomerates. While state-owned entities attempt to democratize access through low-cost editions, challenges such as the digital divide, the stigma associating reading with educational obligation, and the high cost of alternative books limit their impact on the general population. This situation is highly relevant to open data because it demonstrates how official statistics can inform cultural policy, yet also reveals limitations in how metrics are defined and collected. The discrepancy between declining reported readership and the rise of digital consumption patterns suggests that traditional data collection methods may be outdated. For open data initiatives, this case emphasizes the need to update indicators to include digital formats and ensure that policy responses are based on comprehensive, contemporary evidence that truly reflects changing citizen behaviors.
