Los datos personales no son una cuestión del primer mundo | La República EC

Although Ecuador has recently enacted data protection laws and constitutional rulings, these measures remain insufficient to address the root causes of unauthorized data processing. The legislation represents a significant victory for individual liberties, yet its practical impact is limited because it does not fully prevent or solve the systemic issues behind privacy violations. The mere existence of norms has not translated into effective protection against the misuse of personal information, leaving a gap between legal frameworks and real-world application. The persistence of this problem stems from a profound lack of awareness among citizens and entities regarding data rights. Many individuals do not understand when their privacy is being violated, preventing them from seeking legal recourse, while companies often exploit standard consent forms to claim unlimited rights over user data. This mutual misunderstanding creates an environment where constitutional rights are effectively waived through opaque agreements, reinforcing a culture where personal information is treated as a commodity rather than a fundamental right deserving strict protection. This article is crucial for open data initiatives because it highlights that technical transparency and data availability are meaningless without robust legal safeguards and public literacy. Open data practices must evolve beyond mere disclosure to ensure that individuals retain control and understanding of their information. Without addressing the human and legal dimensions of data consent, the open data movement risks exacerbating privacy breaches rather than fostering trust. Ultimately, protecting the "life project" of individuals requires recognizing that data privacy is a fundamental human right, integral to the ethical foundation of any open data ecosystem.
