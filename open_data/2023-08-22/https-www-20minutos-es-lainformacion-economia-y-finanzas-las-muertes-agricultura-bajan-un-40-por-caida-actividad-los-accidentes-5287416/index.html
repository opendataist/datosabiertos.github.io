The article reports a significant reduction in agricultural worker fatalities in Spain during the first half of 2023 compared to the previous year. While union representatives acknowledge this statistical improvement, they urge caution against declaring it a definitive trend. They attribute the decrease primarily to anomalous weather conditions, specifically severe drought, which reduced labor demand and field activities rather than reflecting a substantial systemic safety overhaul. This context highlights the difficulty of distinguishing between cyclical fluctuations and genuine progress in occupational health within seasonal industries. Despite the lower casualty rates, experts emphasize that fundamental structural risks remain unaddressed. These include precarious employment conditions, high worker turnover, inadequate initial safety training, and outdated machinery that fails to meet current European safety standards. Although new government campaigns and specific regulations on extreme temperatures have been implemented, they are deemed insufficient to guarantee safety. The sector continues to struggle with complex monitoring challenges, such as the isolation of work sites, the prevalence of self-employed workers, and language barriers among foreign labor, which hinder effective risk prevention. The potential for lasting improvement lies in the generational shift towards younger, better-trained workers and the gradual integration of new technologies that reduce exposure to hazardous tasks. This narrative is relevant to open data because it demonstrates that statistical trends must be interpreted alongside qualitative contextual factors. To accurately assess public health and safety outcomes, open data initiatives must integrate multidimensional variables—such as environmental conditions and labor market dynamics—rather than relying solely on raw incident counts, ensuring that data-driven insights reflect the true complexity of occupational safety.
