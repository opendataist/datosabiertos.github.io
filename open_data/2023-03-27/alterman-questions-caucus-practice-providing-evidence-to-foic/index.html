Alterman questions caucus practice, providing evidence to FOIC

Norwalk city officials defended their practice of inviting non-council members, including staff and legislators, to Democratic caucus meetings, arguing that such gatherings remain exempt from the Freedom of Information Act as long as outsiders do not participate in debates. This stance follows controversy over a postponed council retreat where state representatives engaged in dialogue with council members, prompting allegations that the majority violated open government norms. The city cited informal guidance from the Freedom of Information Commission, asserting that passive attendance does not transform a private caucus into a public meeting. However, the legal basis for this interpretation remains contentious, as the guidance provided came from a commission staff member rather than the commission itself, which holds the authority to issue binding rulings. Critics argue that inviting legislators and engaging in substantive policy discussions contradicts the strict limitations required for the caucus exception. The disagreement highlights the ambiguity in how "participation" is defined, with officials claiming adherence to longstanding tradition while opponents view the expanded access as a mechanism to bypass public scrutiny. This dispute is critical to open data and transparency advocates because it illustrates the persistent challenges in enforcing access to government deliberations. When legislative bodies exploit procedural loopholes to exclude the public, it undermines accountability and limits the availability of public records regarding decision-making processes. The case underscores the need for clearer statutory definitions of public versus private meetings to ensure that significant policy discussions, such as those regarding municipal funding and housing, remain accessible to citizens rather than confined to closed-door sessions.
