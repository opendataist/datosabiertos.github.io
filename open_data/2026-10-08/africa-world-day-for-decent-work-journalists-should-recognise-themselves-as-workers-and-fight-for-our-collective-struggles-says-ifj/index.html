Africa: World Day for Decent Work - 'Journalists Should Recognise Themselves As Workers and Fight for Our Collective Struggles,' Says IFJ

Africa: World Day for Decent Work - 'Journalists Should Recognise Themselves As Workers and Fight for Our Collective Struggles,' Says IFJ

Media workers globally face severe deterioration in working conditions, characterized by inadequate pay, long hours, and precarious employment that undermines their ability to serve democracy. This systemic insecurity leads to significant health issues, including high rates of stress and depression, and causes many professionals to consider leaving the industry. These findings highlight a critical gap between the ideal of decent work and the reality faced by journalists who often lack basic contracts and fair remuneration. The article demonstrates that organized collective action through unions is the most effective mechanism for reversing these trends and securing workers' rights. Successful cases across various countries show that strikes and joint negotiations can force employers to reverse detrimental decisions, restore wages, and protect essential media services from closure. These victories underscore the necessity of strong collective agreements and the legal backing required to defend journalists against unilateral management changes and harassment. This issue is highly relevant to open data because transparent information regarding labor conditions is essential for advocating policy changes and supporting civil society. Open data initiatives can help document and visualize the prevalence of unfair labor practices, thereby amplifying calls for regulatory oversight and ethical standards in the media sector. By making data on journalist welfare accessible, stakeholders can better identify trends, hold organizations accountable, and promote a media environment where workers have the security needed to inform the public independently and safely.
