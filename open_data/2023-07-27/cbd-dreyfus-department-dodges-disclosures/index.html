CBD: Dreyfus’ department dodges disclosures

The article highlights a significant failure in Australia’s freedom of information system, where government departments can easily obstruct public access to sensitive information under the guise of protecting national interests. This lack of transparency was exposed when a newspaper’s request regarding allegations of torture at detention centers was met with delays and obfuscation, requiring the intervention of the Information Commissioner to enforce compliance. Such behavior undermines public trust and demonstrates a systemic unwillingness to uphold accountability in matters of serious human rights concern. Beyond this specific incident, the text critiques broader institutional failures, including poor financial governance within professional bodies and negligent data handling practices. These examples illustrate how organizations often prioritize reputation management or operational inefficiency over ethical standards and data security. The recurring theme is a disconnect between the stated values of transparency and the actual practices of those in power, suggesting a cultural problem that extends beyond individual malfunctions to systemic negligence. This is critically relevant to open data because it demonstrates the real-world consequences of opaque government operations. When entities hide information or mishandle data, it erodes the foundation of trust necessary for a functioning open society. For open data advocates, this serves as a stark reminder that technical availability of data is insufficient without strong legal frameworks and active enforcement to prevent authorities from suppressing information or failing to protect citizen privacy.
