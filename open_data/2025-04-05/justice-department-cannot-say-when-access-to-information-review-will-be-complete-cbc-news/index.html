Justice Department cannot say when access to information review will be complete | CBC News

Justice Department cannot say when access to information review will be complete | CBC News

The ongoing review of Nova Scotia’s Freedom of Information and Protection of Privacy Act highlights the tension between governmental efficiency and public transparency. With the final report delayed until fall, the process underscores how prolonged reviews can hinder immediate access to information, leaving citizens reliant on formal requests that suffer from significant backlogs. This delay impacts the timely delivery of justice for those seeking accountability, suggesting that procedural caution may inadvertently prioritize administrative comfort over open data principles. The political context reveals a concerning pattern where campaign promises of enhanced transparency are diluted once in power. This erosion of trust suggests that without robust, independent oversight mechanisms, governments may resist the very transparency they once pledged to establish, weakening the democratic utility of open data frameworks. Recent legislative attempts to expand officials' power to dismiss requests as frivolous further threaten information accessibility. Although these measures were tempered by public outcry and criticism from the outgoing privacy commissioner, they signal a potential systemic effort to filter or limit data disclosure. This article is relevant to open data because it illustrates how political shifts and administrative gatekeeping can undermine the fundamental principle that public information should be readily available, emphasizing the critical need for strong, independent oversight to protect transparency rights.
