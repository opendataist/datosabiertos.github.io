Sin independencia, nuevos órganos en Ley de Transparencia

Sin independencia, nuevos órganos en Ley de Transparencia

The article highlights a critical shift in Mexico’s transparency framework, arguing that the new General Transparency Law undermines institutional independence by centralizing appointment powers within the Executive branch. Former IFAL commissioner María Marván warns that creating multiple specialized bodies, including "Transparencia para el Pueblo," replaces the previous unified model with a structure susceptible to political interference and conflicts of interest. This concentration of authority risks subordinated oversight, where reviewers depend directly on those they are meant to supervise, effectively weakening the checks and balances essential for democratic accountability. Critics emphasize that the reform eliminates essential citizen protections, such as free and rapid access to review resources, replacing them with costly and lengthy judicial processes. The dissolution of the former autonomous institute discards decades of expertise and operational experience, supposedly for administrative savings that critics deem negligible. By dispersing responsibilities among eighteen different federal authorities and increasing the causes for withholding information, the system creates significant obstacles for citizens seeking public data, thereby diminishing the practical guarantee of access rights. This development is highly relevant to open data advocates because it signals a regression from a robust, independent data governance model to one prioritizing state control over public transparency. The loss of a centralized, autonomous body and the introduction of barriers to accessing information threaten the integrity and availability of public data. For open data initiatives, this represents a systemic risk where data disclosure becomes contingent on political favor rather than legal obligation, potentially stifling civic engagement and independent analysis of government actions.
