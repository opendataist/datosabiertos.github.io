The Government's Antipathy Towards Transparency Has Made FOIA Lawsuits The Default Process

The article highlights a systemic failure in the Freedom of Information Act, where government agencies routinely ignore requests for transparency, forcing citizens to rely on litigation to access public records. Rather than voluntarily disclosing information, agencies involved in law enforcement and counterterrorism increasingly stall, resist, or completely ignore FOIA requests. This behavior transforms the legal requirement for openness into a barrier, effectively shielding opaque operations from public scrutiny until a court mandates compliance. The core issue is the perversion of the FOIA process, which was designed as a tool for accountability rather than a last-resort remedy. By treating lawsuits as a standard procedural step rather than an exception, agencies exploit the limited resources of ordinary citizens, knowing that not everyone can afford to fight for disclosure. This creates a scenario where government operations remain hidden simply because the public lacks the financial means to challenge non-compliance, undermining the democratic ideal of an informed electorate. This dynamic is critically relevant to open data because it reveals that legal mandates for transparency are insufficient without proactive institutional willingness to share information. When agencies treat open data initiatives as burdensome obligations to be litigated rather than inherent duties, the resulting data ecosystem remains incomplete and skewed. True open data requires removing these procedural obstacles, ensuring that information is accessible and usable without the threat of expensive legal battles, thereby restoring genuine accountability and trust in public institutions.
