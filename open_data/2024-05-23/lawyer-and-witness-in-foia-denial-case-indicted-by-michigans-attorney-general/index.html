Lawyer and Witness in FOIA Denial Case Indicted by Michigan’s Attorney General

This case highlights the critical tension between government transparency and claims of cybersecurity in the realm of election data. A legal battle in Michigan emerged when a resident used a Freedom of Information Act request to access electronic poll book records, revealing discrepancies between local voting logs and the state’s official voter file. The resulting prosecution of the lawyer and a township clerk who refused to delete these records suggests that officials may be prioritizing control over data integrity rather than ensuring accurate, auditable election processes. The prosecution’s actions, including charging individuals for preserving evidence and analyzing it, raise serious concerns about the suppression of information necessary for public oversight. The defense argues that these charges target transparency and truth-telling, effectively silencing those who question the accuracy of state-held voter rolls. This dynamic implies that without independent access to raw election data, citizens and experts cannot adequately verify the integrity of electoral outcomes or identify potential systematic errors. This incident is highly relevant to open data advocates because it demonstrates how broad assertions of privacy and security can be leveraged to deny public access to essential records. Electronic poll books contain unique audit trails that are often absent from centralized state databases, yet they are being withheld or destroyed under state directives. If such data remains inaccessible, it becomes impossible for the public to confirm that elections are conducted fairly, underscoring the urgent need for policies that mandate the release and preservation of detailed, machine-readable election records.
