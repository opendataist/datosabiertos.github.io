Can't share machine-readable voter list as it may lead to breach of privacy: CEC Gyanesh Kumar

Can't share machine-readable voter list as it may lead to breach of privacy: CEC Gyanesh Kumar

The Election Commission of India maintains a strict prohibition on releasing machine-readable voter lists, prioritizing the privacy and security of citizens over political demands for such data. This stance is rooted in a Supreme Court verdict from 2019, which deemed these formats risky because they allow potential tampering with entries and unauthorized distribution of personal photographs. By distinguishing these unsafe formats from secure, searchable online records, the Commission emphasizes that voter privacy remains paramount and cannot be compromised for partisan convenience or transparency arguments that ignore data protection risks. Simultaneously, the Commission has enhanced accessibility and transparency through the adoption of specific Supreme Court directives regarding electoral roll revisions. By publishing detailed lists of proposed deletions along with reasons and establishing platforms for citizens to search their status or file claims, the process is made more accountable. This approach allows aggrieved voters to rectify errors efficiently using digital tools and Aadhaar verification, ensuring that while sensitive data is restricted, the integrity of the voter list is maintained through a transparent, collaborative administrative framework rather than blanket data publication. This development is highly relevant to open data discussions as it illustrates the critical tension between transparency and privacy. It demonstrates that "open data" does not universally apply to personal information; instead, it requires nuanced policies that balance public accountability with individual rights. The case highlights how open data frameworks must evolve to distinguish between actionable, non-identifiable administrative transparency and invasive, machine-readable personal datasets, ensuring that digital governance reforms do not inadvertently erode fundamental privacy protections.
