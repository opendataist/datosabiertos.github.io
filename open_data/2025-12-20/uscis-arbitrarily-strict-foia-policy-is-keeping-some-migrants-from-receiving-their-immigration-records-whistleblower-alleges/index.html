USCIS’ arbitrarily strict FOIA policy is keeping some migrants from receiving their immigration records, whistleblower alleges

USCIS claims a dramatic reduction in its Freedom of Information Act backlog, but internal disclosures suggest this achievement stems from aggressive procedural maneuvers rather than genuine processing improvements. By implementing stricter criteria for request validation, the agency has summarily closed thousands of cases for minor technicalities, such as name mismatches or the use of legal representatives, effectively removing them from pending inventories without thorough review. This approach raises serious concerns about whether the agency is meeting its legal obligations or simply manipulating metrics to appear compliant while denying access to essential records. The practical implications of these policies create a profound information imbalance for immigrants facing deportation proceedings. While government attorneys retain full access to case files, many individuals are denied the very documents they submitted or are told their records do not exist, despite the agency’s possession of them. This lack of transparency prevents noncitizens from adequately preparing their defenses, leading to unjust deportations, prolonged detention, and family separations. The whistleblower argues that these barriers are not merely administrative inefficiencies but structural failures that threaten public safety and due process by leaving vulnerable populations in the dark about their own legal histories. This article is critically relevant to open data because it highlights how institutional opacity can undermine transparency initiatives even when official metrics suggest progress. It demonstrates that without robust oversight and adherence to the spirit of access laws, data releases can be rendered meaningless through restrictive interpretation and technical rejection. The case serves as a cautionary tale for open data advocates, illustrating that true openness requires not just the availability of records, but also accessible, equitable mechanisms for citizens to retrieve their own information without facing insurmountable bureaucratic hurdles.
