Augusta County wants recording of March 20 closed session treated as 'privileged'

The article highlights a legal battle in Augusta County where local officials attempted to classify a legally recorded closed-door meeting as privileged to evade public disclosure. Despite prior acknowledgment that the board member’s one-party consent recording was lawful, counsel argued that the existence of other exempt topics allowed the entire record to be withheld. This stance raises significant concerns for open data advocates, as it suggests a loophole where public bodies could shield routine government business from scrutiny simply by embedding it within discussions about legally exempt matters. This argument threatens the fundamental purpose of Freedom of Information laws by potentially allowing governments to discuss non-exempt business behind closed doors if they can claim any portion of the meeting involved privileged topics. If accepted, this interpretation would severely undermine transparency, enabling public entities to circumvent open meeting requirements and deny citizens access to critical records. The core implication is that the integrity of public oversight depends on strict adherence to what topics truly require closure, rather than allowing broad exemptions to swallow the rule of open governance. The relevance to open data lies in the preservation of public records as accessible resources for accountability. When legal interpretations allow entire datasets or recordings to be redacted or withheld based on partial exemptions, the utility of open data is compromised. Ensuring that legally obtained records remain subject to FOIA prevents the government from controlling the narrative through selective secrecy. Ultimately, this case underscores the necessity of robust legal frameworks that prioritize transparency and ensure that open data initiatives are not rendered ineffective by restrictive administrative interpretations.
