Documents reveal plans to make 39,000 hectares of native Tasmanian forest available for logging

The release of right-to-information documents reveals that nearly 40,000 hectares of native Tasmanian forest, previously classified as protected future reserve land, have been reclassified for potential logging. This shift allows the government to clear these areas at short notice, threatening significant biodiversity including old-growth ecosystems and habitats for threatened species like the masked owl and wedge-tailed eagle. The secrecy surrounding these locations highlights a lack of transparency in environmental decision-making, contradicting public commitments to conservation. The primary driver for this expansion is the public forestry company’s urgent need for high-quality saw logs to sustain regional mills, which face a looming supply deficit from native forests. By repurposing land designated for future protection, the state aims to secure timber volumes that plantations cannot currently provide. This move underscores the tension between industrial economic demands and ecological preservation, suggesting that short-term industry survival is prioritized over long-term environmental stewardship. This situation is highly relevant to open data because it demonstrates how withholding critical geographical and ecological information prevents public scrutiny and accountability. The successful challenge by BirdLife Australia to release these documents illustrates the vital role of transparency initiatives in exposing hidden environmental risks. It serves as a powerful case study on the necessity of open data in holding governments accountable for decisions that impact public lands and biodiversity.
