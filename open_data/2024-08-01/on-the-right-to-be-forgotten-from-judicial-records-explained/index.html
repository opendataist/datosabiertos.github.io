On the ‘right to be forgotten’ from judicial records | Explained

The Supreme Court’s review of the "right to be forgotten" regarding judicial records highlights a critical tension between individual privacy and the fundamental principle of open justice. This case challenges whether acquitted individuals can demand the removal of public court documents from legal databases, a request that conflicts with the transparency required for a functioning democratic society. The Court’s caution against blanket removals underscores the serious implications for public access to legal history and the integrity of public records. For open data advocates, this ruling defines the boundaries of data erasure in the public sector. It questions whether statutory privacy laws override the legal obligation of courts to maintain accessible archives. If private entities like Indian Kanoon are forced to delete judgments based on individual privacy claims, it sets a precedent that could fragment the public legal domain, making it difficult to ensure consistent, complete, and unbiased access to judicial outcomes for researchers and the public. The absence of clear statutory guidelines places an undue burden on private platforms to balance conflicting rights, risking arbitrary censorship or incomplete data availability. Open data principles rely on standardized, predictable access; however, ad-hoc judicial orders create a fragmented landscape where information availability depends on individual litigation rather than transparent policy. This uncertainty threatens the reliability of legal data ecosystems and necessitates robust, uniform frameworks that protect privacy without compromising the collective right to information.
