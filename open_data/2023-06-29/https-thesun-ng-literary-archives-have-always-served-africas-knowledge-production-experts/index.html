The discussion underscores the critical role of literary archives in generating indigenous African knowledge, challenging historical narratives that dismissed the continent’s cultural depth. By producing sophisticated literature in local languages, African scholars and writers have created essential counter-discourses to colonial erasure, establishing epistemological frameworks that validate indigenous governance, religion, and social structures. This literary production is not merely artistic but serves as a foundational pillar for reclaiming intellectual sovereignty and documenting complex societal nuances that Western archives often overlooked. However, the conversation highlights a significant paradox in how African languages are treated within academic systems, where experts in African languages are often trained exclusively in European tongues. This double standard limits the ability to engage deeply with indigenous traditions and suggests that fundamental educational reform is necessary to integrate local languages into curricula. Such integration would not only correct historical imbalances but also create new skills and social mobility, ensuring that knowledge production remains rooted in the linguistic realities of the people it describes. Relevance to open data lies in the urgent need to digitize and preserve these indigenous linguistic and literary records before they vanish. Many African languages face imminent extinction, meaning that valuable cultural and intellectual data is at risk of permanent loss. Open data initiatives must prioritize the archiving and accessibility of these materials in their original languages, ensuring that future research can draw upon a diverse, multilingual dataset. This preservation effort is vital for creating a truly inclusive global knowledge infrastructure that respects and utilizes the full spectrum of African intellectual heritage.
