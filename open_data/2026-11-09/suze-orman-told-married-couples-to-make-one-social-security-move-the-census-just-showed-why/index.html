Suze Orman Told Married Couples to Make One Social Security Move. The Census Just Showed Why

This analysis highlights a critical vulnerability for married couples: the risk of falling into poverty after one spouse dies, particularly if they failed to optimize Social Security claiming strategies. While nearly half of all low-income retiree households are single individuals, the data serves as a stark warning for married couples who do not plan for the transition to widowhood. The core insight is that household financial stability in later years is often determined by the surviving spouse’s income, which depends entirely on the benefit amount the higher earner secured. The most significant factor in this outcome is the decision of when the higher earner claims benefits. By delaying claims until age 70, couples maximize the survivor benefit, which the surviving spouse receives entirely. Conversely, early claiming significantly reduces this lifelong income stream, potentially pushing the survivor below the poverty line even if they possessed substantial other assets. The delay acts as an insurance policy against the sharp drop in household income that occurs when a spouse passes away, preventing many from joining the millions of retirees living on less than two thousand dollars a month. This situation is highly relevant to open data because it demonstrates how public statistics, such as Census poverty rates, can inform personal financial planning and policy advocacy. By making income and demographic trends transparent, open data empowers individuals to understand systemic risks and make informed decisions about retirement security. It underscores the importance of accessible, reliable data in helping citizens navigate complex financial landscapes and highlights where educational interventions might prevent widespread economic hardship among the aging population.
