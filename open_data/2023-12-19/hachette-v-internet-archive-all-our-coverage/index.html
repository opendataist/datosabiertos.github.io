Hachette v. Internet Archive: All Our Coverage

The legal battle over the Internet Archive’s "controlled digital lending" program has effectively ended with the decision not to appeal a Second Circuit ruling that declared the practice copyright infringement. This conclusion solidifies the stance that scanning and lending digitized copies of physical books without publisher permission constitutes a violation of copyright law. The rejection of the fair use defense marks a significant setback for the Archive, removing the legal justification for its widely used method of providing digital access to licensed works. This outcome has profound implications for open data and digital preservation efforts, particularly regarding the tension between copyright enforcement and public access to information. By invalidating controlled digital lending, the ruling suggests that large-scale digitization and dissemination of copyrighted materials face steep legal barriers. This creates uncertainty for institutions relying on similar models for archival preservation and free educational access, forcing a reevaluation of how digital collections can be legally managed and shared without infringing on intellectual property rights. The case highlights the critical importance of clear legal frameworks for open data initiatives that intersect with commercial content. As digital libraries and archives strive to expand access, they must navigate complex copyright landscapes that may prioritize commercial interests over public benefit. This precedent encourages stakeholders to seek alternative models for open access, emphasizing the need for robust licensing agreements or statutory reforms that better balance the rights of creators with the mission of libraries to provide widespread informational resources.
