El dinero no cunde

The article highlights that Spain’s inflation has shifted from a moderate trend to a persistent, structural problem driven by soaring energy and raw material costs. This surge erodes purchasing power fundamentally, as underlying inflation remains high and affects more than just volatile items like food and fuel. Consequently, money buys less, threatening the financial stability of households after years of accumulated crises. This situation is particularly relevant to open data because understanding and monitoring such complex economic shifts requires transparent, accessible, and timely statistical information. Open data enables citizens, researchers, and policymakers to verify government claims, track the real impact of inflation on different demographics, and hold authorities accountable. Without high-quality, publicly available datasets, it is impossible to accurately assess whether policies are working or if the economic pain is worsening, especially in a globalized market where traditional governance tools struggle. Ultimately, the text argues that without effective price containment, salary increases become essential to preserve purchasing power, though they carry inflationary risks. The persistence of high inflation suggests current measures are insufficient, and elections may further delay effective solutions. Therefore, relying on robust, open economic data is crucial for debating these trade-offs. It empowers society to demand better governance and ensures that decisions regarding salaries and subsidies are based on clear evidence rather than political expediency, fostering a more resilient and informed public discourse.
