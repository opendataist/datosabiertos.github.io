Privacy at risk: Finland's biometric data usage proposal goes too far

Privacy at risk: Finland's biometric data usage proposal goes too far

The proposed expansion of biometric data reuse in Finland is fundamentally flawed because it subjects a vast majority of the population to surveillance without demonstrable necessity. Although proponents claim this measure enhances public safety, statistical evidence reveals that only a negligible fraction of criminal cases would actually benefit from such broad data processing. This disparity highlights a severe lack of proportionality, where the potential infringement on individual privacy vastly outweighs the tangible security benefits, violating core principles of data protection law. From an open data perspective, this article underscores the critical importance of the principles of necessity and purpose limitation when managing public sector data. The proposal’s failure to conduct a comprehensive Data Protection Impact Assessment raises serious concerns about governance and accountability in data reuse. Without rigorous risk assessments and clear legal justifications, the expansion of biometric processing sets a dangerous precedent for arbitrary data collection, eroding trust in public institutions and undermining the ethical frameworks that should guide the handling of sensitive information. Ultimately, the article serves as a stark warning against prioritizing security efficiency over fundamental rights. It argues that true open data governance requires strict adherence to legal standards and transparent oversight mechanisms rather than vague statutory expansions. By comparing Finland’s approach to stricter international models, the author illustrates that effective crime prevention does not require sacrificing privacy. This reinforces the open data imperative that data reuse must always be justified, limited, and safeguarded to prevent the erosion of civil liberties in the name of administrative convenience.
