When this COVID-19 quarantine hub was used for flood victims, some felt 'unsafe' amid violence and drug use

The article highlights significant operational failures in the federal Centre for National Resilience, which was hastily repurposed to house Victorian flood victims. Freedom of Information documents reveal a pattern of severe behavioral incidents, including drug trafficking, family violence, and self-harm threats, indicating that the facility struggled to manage residents with complex needs. This chaos stemmed partly from a rushed admission process that lacked adequate vetting, allowing some individuals who were not affected by the floods to access emergency housing, thereby compromising the safety and welfare of legitimate victims. In contrast, local recovery centers near residents' original homes received positive feedback for fostering community support and stability. Experts emphasize that keeping survivors within their existing social networks is crucial for long-term disaster recovery, as it builds social capital and aids in the rebuilding process. The stark difference in outcomes suggests that geographic proximity and community integration are more effective than centralized, isolated facilities, which can exacerbate behavioral issues and disconnect individuals from their support systems. This case is highly relevant to open data advocacy because it demonstrates how transparency through Freedom of Information requests can expose systemic governance failures in crisis management. By making incident reports public, stakeholders can critically assess the efficacy of emergency responses and hold agencies accountable. This underscores the necessity of accessible, transparent data in public service delivery, ensuring that future emergency accommodations are designed based on evidence and actual resident welfare rather than logistical convenience alone.
