Two FOI bills would destroy the public's right to know

The article condemns proposed legislative changes in Arkansas that threaten to significantly erode the public’s right to access government information. By allowing secret board meetings and redefining what constitutes a public gathering, these bills aim to obscure decision-making processes from citizens. This shift prioritizes administrative convenience over transparency, potentially enabling corrupt practices to go unnoticed until they are already established. These measures are criticized for imposing excessive fees and broad exemptions that withhold drafts, notes, and internal communications from public scrutiny. Experts argue that such restrictions disproportionately affect ordinary taxpayers who already fund these services, effectively locking them out of understanding policy formation before final decisions are made. The result is a substantial reduction in civic engagement, as citizens lose the early insight necessary to influence or challenge government actions effectively. This issue is critically relevant to open data because it highlights the ongoing struggle to maintain accessible, transparent public records as a foundation for accountability. When governments classify preliminary documents as exempt or charge prohibitive fees, they violate the core principles of open data: that information produced with public funds should be freely available. Protecting these rights ensures that data remains a tool for democratic empowerment rather than a mechanism for political secrecy.
