The approval of Spain’s Transparency Law has transformed access to public information, shifting the paradigm from a restricted right to a universal one. However, ten years later, the culture of transparency within public administrations remains insufficient, hampered by a lack of resources and sluggishness in responses. This means that, although the legal framework exists, its practical application lacks the effectiveness needed to fully guarantee citizens’ right to information. To improve this situation, it is urgent to reform the law by prioritizing proactive disclosure as the most efficient means, and by granting the Transparency Council coercive powers to enforce compliance with its rulings. The absence of such enforcement mechanisms significantly limits the Council’s ability to sanction administrative non-compliance, leaving many rulings unenforced and weakening accountability in public management. This situation is relevant to open data because it demonstrates that mere data availability is not enough; strong institutional frameworks with enforceability are required to ensure that information is accurate, up-to-date, and accessible. Without mechanisms that compel administrations to proactively publish information and respond promptly, efforts to democratize information collide with bureaucratic resistance, underscoring the need for a more robust and autonomous data governance system.
