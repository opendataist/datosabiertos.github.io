Risk of electoral fraud. Are you sure that only 163,355 people were naturalized under the 'Grandchildren's Law'? The figure came from the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and the National Statistics Institute.

The Supreme Court has upheld the suspension of voting rights for individuals naturalized under the controversial provisions of the so-called “Grandchildren’s Law,” drastically limiting this group’s electoral participation. This judicial decision generates significant uncertainty regarding the legitimacy of the current electoral register, as it is estimated that the vast majority of those benefiting from this administrative pathway lack the necessary requirements to vote, which could dramatically alter the outcomes in the upcoming general elections. The relevance of this case for open data lies in the opacity and the potential statistical manipulation underlying the matter. The initial figure used by the Government to identify those affected was based on cross-referencing data between the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and the Electoral Census Office. However, the lack of transparency regarding the exact origin of these records, along with the alleged leak of the legal instruction, calls into question the integrity and accessibility of reliable public data—essential elements for ensuring fair and auditable electoral processes. This situation exposes vulnerabilities in the management of administrative data when it intersects with fundamental political rights. If census data do not accurately reflect citizens’ legal status, confidence in democratic institutions is compromised. Critical analysis of these data sources is crucial for detecting anomalies and ensuring that political decisions are based on truthful, accessible, and verifiable information, thereby avoiding biased interpretations that could influence the sovereign electorate.
