CT lawmakers are weighing changes to FOIA. What would they do?

Proposed legislation in Connecticut seeks to significantly restrict public access to government records, creating new exemptions for university research materials, police body camera footage, and state employees’ home addresses. Supporters, including public employee unions and law enforcement, argue these changes are essential to protect individuals from harassment, privacy invasions, and interference with scholarly work. They contend that the current broad interpretation of transparency laws exposes workers to undue risk and distracts from their public duties. Opponents, comprising civil liberties groups and transparency advocates, warn that these measures undermine democratic accountability by shielding public institutions from scrutiny. They emphasize that taxpayer-funded entities, such as public universities and police departments, must remain open to public inspection to ensure integrity. Furthermore, critics note that such legal restrictions offer limited practical protection in the digital age, where personal information is often already accessible through other public channels like property and voter records. This debate is critically relevant to open_data as it highlights the ongoing tension between data privacy and the public’s right to access government information. The proposed exclusions threaten to erode the foundational principles of transparency that enable journalists and citizens to hold officials accountable. If passed, these changes could set a precedent for limiting data availability across various sectors, potentially reducing the transparency necessary for an informed democracy and hindering the public’s ability to analyze government performance and conduct.
