The article reveals a systemic failure by the City of Davenport to enforce building codes, directly contributing to a catastrophic building collapse. Despite years of documented substandard conditions, including a deteriorating load-bearing wall and non-functional heating, city officials halted re-inspections and allowed tenants to remain in unsafe units. This negligence occurred while the city continued to process federal housing payments for these residents, prioritizing administrative compliance over physical safety. This case highlights critical vulnerabilities in the administration of open government data and public accountability. The stark contrast between the city’s internal code enforcement records, which cited dozens of violations, and the clean HUD inspections used to justify continued funding exposes significant data integrity issues. It demonstrates how siloed information systems and a lack of transparent, accessible public records can obscure critical safety risks from oversight bodies and the public. The relevance to open data lies in the urgent need for interoperable, transparent datasets that connect housing subsidies with safety inspections. When data is fragmented or withheld, as seen in Davenport, it enables officials to bypass mandatory checks and maintain fraudulent compliance records. Ensuring that inspection data, financial aid records, and code enforcement reports are publicly accessible and integrated is essential for preventing similar tragedies and holding municipal authorities accountable for the safety of vulnerable populations.
