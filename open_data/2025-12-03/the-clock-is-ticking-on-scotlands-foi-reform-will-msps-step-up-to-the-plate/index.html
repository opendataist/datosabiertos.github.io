The clock is ticking on Scotland’s FoI reform. Will MSPs step up to the plate?

The clock is ticking on Scotland’s FoI reform. Will MSPs step up to the plate?

The Scottish freedom of information system is suffering from systemic drift, relying on goodwill rather than enforceable legal structures. This weakness allows public bodies to evade transparency through delay, selective compliance, and the destruction of critical records. The recent exposure of these failures, particularly regarding record-keeping under ministerial oversight, demonstrates that the current framework no longer guarantees accountability or public trust, creating a significant risk to democratic integrity. At the core of this dysfunction is the First Ministerial veto, which enables ministers to override independent transparency decisions. Critics argue this mechanism undermines Scotland’s international reputation and conflicts with strong public demand for genuine openness. Reform efforts aim to remove this power, shifting control from political discretion to democratic oversight. By ensuring that statutory duties and consequences are legally binding rather than optional, the system would better reflect the expectation that public institutions remain answerable to citizens. This transition is urgent as trust in government reaches historic lows, yet legislative windows are closing rapidly. The proposed reform bill seeks to modernize the law by extending coverage, strengthening enforcement, and mandating proper record-keeping. For open data advocates, this moment is critical because it highlights the difference between nominal access and actual transparency. Strengthening these mechanisms ensures that data and information remain accessible, preserved, and usable for public scrutiny, rather than vanishing under bureaucratic or political pressure.
