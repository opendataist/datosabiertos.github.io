“Números de animales de compañía de acuerdo con el censo, no reflejan la realidad de los hogares”

The 2023 National Census in Uruguay reveals a stark discrepancy between official statistics on pet ownership and the urgent reality reported by animal protection organizations. While government data indicates that a significant portion of households own dogs or cats, with specific averages per home, shelter representatives argue that these figures drastically underrepresent the true population. They claim there is an exponential, uncontrolled growth in stray and abandoned animals, suggesting that the census methodology fails to capture the extent of the crisis on the ground, where many families keep multiple pets or abandon animals during housing transitions. This disconnect highlights critical implications for open data transparency and the necessity of cross-verifying official metrics with grassroots evidence. When institutional data diverges significantly from observed social realities, it undermines effective policy-making and resource allocation for animal welfare. The case underscores how open data initiatives must not only collect information but also facilitate dialogue between statistical bodies and civil society to ensure that reported numbers reflect actual conditions, thereby enabling accurate assessments of public health and social welfare challenges. Furthermore, the article reveals that even when regulations exist, such as limits on fireworks decibels or mandates for sterilization in housing plans, enforcement is virtually non-existent. The lack of rigorous monitoring allows for the continued suffering and abandonment of animals, particularly among displaced families. This scenario emphasizes the role of open data in holding authorities accountable; by making discrepancies between law, data, and reality visible, stakeholders can advocate for stricter enforcement and more humane policies, ensuring that digital transparency translates into tangible improvements for vulnerable populations.
