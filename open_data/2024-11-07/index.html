Open Data: Trust, Privacy, and the Risks of Raw Information

Open data gives people direct access to raw information. This helps users check facts on their own. It also builds trust in public institutions. When information is open, people can watch how governments spend money and how companies behave. They do not need middlemen to tell them what is happening.

For example, researchers used open data from FAOSTAT. They tracked global potato production trends themselves. This meant they did not have to rely on reports that might be biased. In Rosario, citizens used open mobility data. They looked at taxi prices and fairness. This showed how easy access to information helps communities hold service providers accountable.

However, this access comes with risks. People often misunderstand raw statistics if they lack context. This can lead to wrong conclusions. The Bermuda Police Service’s 2021 report is a good example. Crime rates dropped, but this was because of pandemic restrictions, not because communities were safer. Raw data alone did not explain this. Also, open datasets can violate privacy. The lawsuit against Clearview AI shows this problem. The company scraped public facial images to train its AI. This raised ethical questions about personal security versus data availability.

The quality of open data depends on the standards used by providers. If data formats are inconsistent, it is hard to analyze. In Nottingham, privatized children’s social care lacked standardized data. This made it hard to compare costs or check for waste. Without uniform data, users cannot make informed decisions about public services.

There is also a conflict between transparency and intellectual property. Big tech companies often scrape copyrighted content to train AI models. They do not pay creators for this. This hurts the income of independent media. Matt Rogerson argues that this breaks the social contract of the open web. Publishers lose control over their work. Users face a choice: use advanced AI tools or support a fair system for creators. Currently, the system favors corporate efficiency over fair pay.

Open data is also a tool for social justice. It reveals trends that big numbers might hide. In Norway, open data showed that immigrants have high life satisfaction and trust in institutions. This is true even though they face economic challenges. This detailed insight helps advocates create better plans. However, users must be careful. They need to tell the difference between real patterns and random noise. This requires good data skills.

Finally, open data helps people engage in their communities. It gives direct access to personal and community records. In Peru, the Provincial Registry offers an open data portal. Researchers and citizens can look at local migration and family structures. They can see birth, death, and marriage records. This helps people make better decisions. But users must balance this benefit with privacy risks. They must ensure that open access does not compromise sensitive personal information.
