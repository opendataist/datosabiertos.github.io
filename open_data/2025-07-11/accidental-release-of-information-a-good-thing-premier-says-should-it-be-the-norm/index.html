Accidental release of information a 'good thing', premier says. Should it be the norm?

Accidental release of information a 'good thing', premier says. Should it be the norm?

The accidental unredacted release of internal staff concerns regarding youth detention in Tasmanian watch houses highlights the tension between government secrecy and public accountability. The Premier’s approval of the disclosure suggests a growing recognition that transparency, even when achieved by error, serves the public interest. This incident challenges the traditional default of withholding information, arguing instead for a system where data is public by default and the government must justify any exclusions. Current Right to Information mechanisms are flawed, relying on broad exemptions like "internal deliberative information" that often suppress legitimate staff feedback and operational issues. These loopholes allow departments to withhold significant amounts of data without providing clear explanations, undermining the spirit of open governance. The case demonstrates how these mechanisms can obscure critical details about public service failures and resource management, preventing meaningful public scrutiny of government operations. This situation is highly relevant to open data advocates because it exposes systemic inefficiencies and the overwork of RTI officers who face mounting pressures with limited resources. The high rate of incorrect withholdings and public dissatisfaction indicates a need for structural reform rather than incremental changes. As Tasmania reviews its transparency laws, this event underscores the urgent necessity to align legal frameworks with modern expectations for openness, ensuring that open data principles are practically implemented rather than merely theorized.
