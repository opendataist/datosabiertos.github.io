The article concludes that Google Chrome’s incognito mode does not provide the full privacy protection it advertises. While it locally clears browsing history, cookies, and form data, this feature offers only temporary and limited privacy. Research reveals that the tool does not encrypt the connection or hide the IP address, meaning complete anonymity is not guaranteed. The implications of this limitation are significant, as external actors such as Internet service providers, network administrators, and Google itself can still track user activity. Moreover, installed extensions and downloads may leave accessible traces. This demonstrates that private browsing on a device does not equate to invisible browsing on the network, challenging the common perception that using this feature ensures absolute confidentiality of personal data. This information is crucial for the open data movement and digital literacy, as it exposes how technology often sells a false sense of security. Transparency about the actual capabilities of privacy tools enables users to make informed decisions and understand that data protection requires a multifaceted approach. Fostering this awareness helps combat technological misinformation and promotes the responsible use of digital tools, which is essential for an empowered and critical civil society.
