Spain and Portugal uniquely preserve the tradition of using two surnames per person, a practice rooted in sixteenth-century social differentiation that historically prioritized the paternal lineage. Although recent regulations now allow parents to choose the order of surnames and adults to modify their registration, the cultural weight of these identifiers remains strong, shaping how individuals are perceived within their communities and families. The recent publication of surname frequency data by the national statistics institute reveals distinct regional patterns across Castilla-La Mancha, with García emerging as the most common name in every province. This granular data highlights not just demographic trends but also the enduring impact of historical naming conventions on modern identity documentation, showing how regional variations reflect broader national characteristics. This article is highly relevant to open data because it demonstrates how public statistical institutions provide transparent, accessible datasets that empower citizens to understand their social structure. By making this information freely available, governments support transparency and allow researchers to analyze historical, cultural, and demographic shifts, turning raw identity data into meaningful insights about societal evolution and heritage.
