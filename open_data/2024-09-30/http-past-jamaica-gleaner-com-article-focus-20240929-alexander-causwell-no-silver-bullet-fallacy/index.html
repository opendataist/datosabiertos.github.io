The article argues against the prevalent bias in policy discourse that favors complex, holistic solutions for perceived complex problems, asserting instead that targeted interventions are often more effective. It contends that the rejection of "silver bullet" approaches is frequently flawed, using historical data on UK drunk driving and current gang violence in Jamaica to demonstrate that focused strategies can yield significant, rapid results. By prioritizing critical dimensions within realistic timeframes, policymakers can avoid the operational paralysis caused by coordinating numerous disparate initiatives. Specifically, the text critiques the extensive spending on social interventions for violence reduction, labeling them largely ineffective compared to structural measures like community formalization and land tenure regularization. It highlights examples from El Salvador, Colombia, and Peru, where pivoting from multifaceted social strategies to targeted security and structural changes drastically reduced murder rates. This approach challenges the assumption that socio-economic factors are the primary drivers of armed violence, suggesting instead that dismantling the physical and organizational safe havens of gangs is the most direct path to safety. This perspective is crucial for open data because it underscores the necessity of precise, outcome-oriented metrics rather than broad, aggregate indicators. Open data initiatives can support this focused policy research by providing granular evidence to evaluate the specific impacts of targeted interventions versus comprehensive social programs. By enabling clear visibility into what actually works, open data helps dismantle the ideological preference for complexity, fostering evidence-based decisions that prioritize efficiency and measurable impact in resource-constrained environments.
