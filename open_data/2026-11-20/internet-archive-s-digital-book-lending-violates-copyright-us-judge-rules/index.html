Internet Archive's digital book lending violates copyright, US judge rules

Internet Archive's digital book lending violates copyright, US judge rules

A U.S. judge ruled that the Internet Archive infringed copyright by scanning and digitally lending millions of books from major publishers without permission. The court rejected the nonprofit’s fair use defense, determining that creating digital copies to replace licensed physical lending constitutes infringement rather than transformative use. This decision clarifies that while owning a physical book allows for lending, it does not grant the right to replicate and distribute digital versions en masse. This ruling is highly relevant to open data and open access advocates because it establishes a legal boundary for digital preservation and distribution of copyrighted cultural heritage. It highlights the tension between ensuring universal access to information and protecting intellectual property rights in the digital age. The verdict impacts how institutions can legally share knowledge, emphasizing that technological capability to digitize does not equate to legal right to distribute. Consequently, libraries and archives must reconsider their strategies for expanding digital access, potentially limiting free availability or seeking explicit licensing. The Internet Archive plans to appeal, arguing the decision harms readers by restricting information flow. Ultimately, the case underscores the need for clear frameworks that balance creator rights with the public interest in widespread educational and cultural access.
