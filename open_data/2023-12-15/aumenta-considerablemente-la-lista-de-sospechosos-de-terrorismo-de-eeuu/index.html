Aumenta considerablemente la lista de sospechosos de terrorismo de EEUU

The US government's terrorist suspect database has expanded exponentially, growing from 120,000 names to nearly two million, with the majority of additions occurring in recent years. This massive accumulation highlights a systemic inefficiency where the volume of data outpaces the capacity for regular review. Consequently, there is a high probability that the list contains outdated entries, including individuals who are deceased, due to insufficient staffing and lack of rigorous maintenance protocols. A critical implication of this data management failure is the severe impact on innocent individuals who are misidentified due to name similarities. Thousands of people face significant life disruptions, such as travel bans and employment issues, yet proving their error is notoriously difficult. The data reveals that nearly all complaints involve mistaken identities, exposing a flawed process where corrective mechanisms are either inaccessible or ineffective, leaving victims without adequate redress despite the government acknowledging the inclusion errors. This article is highly relevant to open_data because it illustrates the urgent need for transparency and accountability in public security datasets. The case demonstrates how opaque, poorly maintained databases can perpetuate civil rights violations and erode public trust. It underscores the importance of open data principles—such as accuracy, verifiability, and the right to correction—to ensure that surveillance systems do not harm citizens and that governmental data practices remain auditable and just.
