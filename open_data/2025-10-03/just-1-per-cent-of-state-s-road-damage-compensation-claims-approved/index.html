Just 1 per cent of state's road damage compensation claims approved

Just 1 per cent of state's road damage compensation claims approved

The article highlights a systemic failure in Victoria’s road infrastructure, where deteriorating conditions and inadequate compensation mechanisms place an unfair burden on citizens. Residents like Lisa Dawson face significant financial losses from damage caused by potholes, only to be denied liability by local councils who claim compliance with maintenance plans. This disconnect between user fees and service delivery creates a sense of injustice, as drivers bear the high costs of repairs while authorities avoid responsibility through legal technicalities and strict procedural thresholds. This issue is critically relevant to open data because the government’s refusal to transparently share incident and claim data initially obscured the scale of the problem. It was only through Freedom of Information requests that journalists uncovered the disparity between the number of reported hazards and the negligible number of successful compensation claims. This data transparency revealed that the vast majority of claims are rejected, proving that official narratives about adequate infrastructure maintenance do not align with the reality experienced by the public. Open data serves as the essential tool for exposing these inefficiencies and holding agencies accountable for public safety. Furthermore, the surge in community-driven reporting via apps like Snap Send Solve indicates a shift toward citizen-led oversight, supplementing traditional governance gaps. The data shows a rising trend in reported defects, yet payouts remain statistically insignificant, suggesting a broken feedback loop in public resource allocation. Ultimately, the article argues that without greater transparency and accessible data regarding road conditions and claim outcomes, the government cannot effectively address the infrastructure crisis or restore public trust in its management of shared public assets.
