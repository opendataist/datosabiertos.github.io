The article highlights systemic corruption within state institutions, where politicians exploit their influence to hire relatives for positions with minimal oversight. This nepotism extends beyond direct legislative contracts, exploiting loopholes that allow family members to secure roles in other agencies while neglecting their duties. Such practices demonstrate how public resources are misused to maintain political control and privilege. This case is relevant to open data because transparency in public hiring and attendance records could expose these discrepancies. By making employment contracts, job descriptions, and daily activity logs publicly accessible, citizens and journalists can verify whether public servants are fulfilling their roles. Open data enables the detection of irregularities such as absenteeism or unqualified appointments, fostering accountability and reducing opportunities for political patronage. The implications suggest that without robust data transparency, anti-nepotism laws remain insufficient. Empowering institutions with accessible data allows for effective monitoring of influence peddling. Ultimately, open data serves as a critical tool for citizens to challenge the dynamic of state appropriation, ensuring that public funds serve the common good rather than private political interests.
