There's More to Copyright Than Financial Incentives, Internet Archive Argues in Court * TorrentFreak

The Internet Archive’s controlled digital lending program, which allows one-to-one borrowing of digitized books, is at the center of a critical legal battle regarding copyright and fair use. After a lower court ruled against the Archive, it is appealing the decision, arguing that its non-profit, access-focused model does not infringe on publishers' rights. This dispute highlights the tension between traditional copyright enforcement and the evolving need to preserve digital history and maintain equitable access to information. The Archive contends that its service is fundamentally different from illegal file-sharing because it restricts access to a single user at a time for each owned physical copy. By maintaining a strict "owned-to-loaned" ratio, the organization argues it mimics traditional library lending without causing financial harm to publishers. Furthermore, the high barrier of entry for digitization prevents a flood of competing services, ensuring that the Archive’s efforts do not disrupt the market but rather provide a necessary alternative to restrictive ebook licensing models. This case is vital to open data and open culture advocates because it tests whether public institutions can preserve and share knowledge freely without being overly constrained by commercial interests. The Archive argues that copyright law should prioritize broad public availability and the preservation of literature over mere financial incentives. A ruling in favor of the Archive would affirm the importance of libraries as egalitarian hubs for knowledge, protecting the public’s right to access cultural heritage in the digital age.
