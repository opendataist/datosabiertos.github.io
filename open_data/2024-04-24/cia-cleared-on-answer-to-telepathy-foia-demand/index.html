CIA Cleared on Answer to Telepathy FOIA Demand

The recent ruling confirms that the Central Intelligence Agency properly managed a Freedom of Information Act request concerning historical claims of psychic espionage. A federal judge determined that the agency conducted thorough searches and acted in good faith, ultimately refusing to produce documents it claimed did not exist or were properly destroyed. This decision validates the use of standard bureaucratic protocols in responding to public records inquiries, even when they involve highly unusual subject matter such as telepathy programs from the 1960s. The case highlights the critical role of the Glomar response in national security transparency. By neither confirming nor denying the existence of certain records, the agency protects sensitive intelligence methods and sources from potential exposure. The court found this approach logical and plausible, noting that acknowledging such a connection could reveal ongoing activities. This precedent reinforces the balance between public accountability and the necessity of safeguarding classified information from scrutiny under open access laws. This article is relevant to open data discussions because it illustrates the limits of transparency when government actions intersect with intelligence operations. It demonstrates how legal frameworks like the FOIA are interpreted to prioritize security over full disclosure, even for seemingly obscure historical requests. Understanding these boundaries is essential for anyone working with government data, as it clarifies why certain datasets may remain inaccessible despite rigorous legal challenges and extensive public interest.
