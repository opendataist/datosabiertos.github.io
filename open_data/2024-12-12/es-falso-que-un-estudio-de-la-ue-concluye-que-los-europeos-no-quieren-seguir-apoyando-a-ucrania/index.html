Es falso que un estudio de la UE concluye que los europeos no quieren seguir apoyando a Ucrania

A video circulating on social media falsely claims that a European Union survey revealed Europeans distrust Ukrainian unity more than Russian unity and oppose continued support for Ukraine. Fact-checkers from Mala Espina and Voz de América debunked these assertions, confirming that no such Eurostat study exists. The organization’s official records contain no mention of this “people’s unity” measurement, and the viral clip lacks the specific visual branding elements, such as the distinctive color ribbon and vertical format, characteristic of genuine Eurostat publications. This misinformation exemplifies the critical need for verifying sources in the digital age, particularly when dealing with politically charged narratives that aim to sway public opinion regarding international conflicts. The rapid spread of unverified statistical claims highlights how easily fabricated content can mimic official data, potentially influencing political discourse and public sentiment without a factual basis. Consumers of information must critically evaluate the provenance of such videos, ensuring they originate from authenticated institutional channels rather than unverified social media messages. The relevance of this case to open data lies in the responsibility of institutions to maintain transparency and the public’s duty to engage with verified information. Open data initiatives aim to provide accessible, reliable statistics for democratic decision-making, making the distinction between authentic data and fabrication essential. When false narratives mimic legitimate open data sources, they erode trust in statistical institutions and obscure the value of accurate information. Therefore, promoting media literacy and robust fact-checking mechanisms are vital to preserving the integrity of open data ecosystems and ensuring that public policy discussions remain grounded in reality rather than disinformation.
