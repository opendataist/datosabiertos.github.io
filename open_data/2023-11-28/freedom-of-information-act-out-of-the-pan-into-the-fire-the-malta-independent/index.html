Freedom of Information Act: out of the pan into the fire? - The Malta Independent

This article critiques Malta’s Freedom of Information Act, arguing that despite its potential to enhance democratic transparency, the legislation remains largely ineffective due to significant structural flaws. The law fails to dismantle the prevailing culture of secrecy within the public administration, as evidenced by prolonged implementation delays and consistent legal victories for the media against government withholding of information. Consequently, the act has not achieved its intended goal of holding the state accountable or preventing maladministration, leaving citizens and journalists with limited practical access to official records. The legislation imposes restrictive barriers that undermine the right to information, including exclusionary residency requirements for foreign journalists and blanket exemptions for key oversight bodies like the Electoral Commission and the Attorney General. These provisions create an unequal playing field where certain stakeholders enjoy greater access than others, contradicting the principle of equal treatment. Furthermore, the law lacks clarity on residency definitions, creating unnecessary bureaucratic hurdles that effectively shield government data from public scrutiny, particularly from external media outlets seeking to report on Maltese affairs. Most critically, the Act compromises judicial independence by allowing the Prime Minister to overrule the Information and Data Protection Commissioner’s decisions to release documents. This provision violates natural justice principles by enabling the executive to act as judge in its own case, prioritizing political interests over transparency and the rule of law. For open data advocates, this case highlights the urgent need for legislative reforms that prioritize unrestricted public access, ensure independent oversight, and align national laws with international standards to genuinely empower civil society and the media.
