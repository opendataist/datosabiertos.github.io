The Supreme Court shields the identity of major rental property owners: you won’t know who is renting to you or at what price

The Supreme Court shields the identity of major rental property owners: you won’t know who is renting to you or at what price

The Supreme Court has set a decisive precedent by shielding the identities of major rental property owners in Catalonia, arguing that public disclosure of their data increases the risk of illegal squatting. This ruling, which could be extended throughout Spain, transforms a previously accessible registry into restricted information, prioritizing public safety over administrative transparency and closing an official channel for identifying who controls the real estate market. For the open data community, this decision represents a significant contraction in access to public information of social interest. By concealing data on housing concentration, it prevents citizen auditing of the market, making it difficult to verify actual prices and asset accumulation. This weakens civil society’s capacity to monitor economic power and expose inequalities, as transparency regarding investment funds and large property holders now depends solely on their willingness to self-disclose. The relevance to the open data community lies in the direct impact on the fight against misinformation and lack of accountability. Without verifiable data, debates on housing policies, such as rent caps, are based on estimates rather than accessible empirical evidence. This case illustrates how security considerations can limit the interoperability and public use of government data, affecting journalistic investigation and the independent analysis necessary for an informed democracy.
