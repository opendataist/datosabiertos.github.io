¿Qué pasará con los bebés nacidos por gestación subrogada tras prohibirse su registro directo en España?

¿Qué pasará con los bebés nacidos por gestación subrogada tras prohibirse su registro directo en España?

A new administrative directive in Spain has substantially changed the procedure for registering children born abroad through surrogacy, a practice that is prohibited within the country. The key change is the outright ban on registering these minors by automatically transcribing foreign birth certificates or court judgments. Instead, families must now establish parentage through standard legal procedures in Spain, primarily requiring a local judicial adoption process, except in cases where a direct biological link permits automatic biological recognition. Although the procedure is now more complex and time-consuming, the child’s immediate well-being remains unaffected. The minor can still travel to Spain with their family, and the adoption process takes place domestically, ensuring that legal recognition is granted in full compliance with Spanish jurisdiction. Pending requests submitted before the directive’s publication are suspended, requiring families to use these new, more rigorous domestic legal channels to secure the child’s legal status. This development is highly relevant to open data, as it highlights the limitations of relying solely on cross-border data transcriptions for legal identity verification. It underscores the need for robust, interoperable data frameworks capable of handling complex, multi-jurisdictional legal statuses without compromising national sovereignty or ethical standards. For open data initiatives, this case illustrates the critical intersection between digital governance, privacy, and international law, emphasizing the need for adaptable data policies that respect both individual rights and local regulatory frameworks when dealing with non-traditional family structures.
