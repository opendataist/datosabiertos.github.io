Judge rules in favor of Augusta County in FOIA case involving March 20 closed session

A recent judicial ruling in Virginia clarifies that recordings made by individual board members for personal use are not subject to Freedom of Information Act requests, as they do not constitute official agency records. The court also upheld the Board of Supervisors’ decision to hold a closed session regarding a fellow member’s resignation, interpreting state law to allow such privacy when the official remains in office and retains the right to revoke their resignation. This decision establishes a precedent where the potential for disciplinary action, even if not formally executed, can legally shield discussions from public scrutiny. This case is critically relevant to open data advocates because it highlights the fragile nature of transparency in local governance. By allowing officials to hide sensitive discussions under the guise of privacy for "resigning" members, the ruling risks normalizing the exclusion of public discourse from democratic oversight. It suggests that unless an election authority formally exercises control over a member, they may evade the mandatory open meeting requirements designed to ensure voters can make informed decisions. Ultimately, this opinion underscores the tension between statutory exemptions and the public’s right to know. While courts aim to protect individual privacy, the broad interpretation of these exemptions can inadvertently serve as a tool for officials to avoid embarrassing or contentious debates. For open data initiatives, this serves as a cautionary tale about the necessity of strict legal definitions and robust enforcement mechanisms to prevent local governments from using procedural loopholes to keep public business behind closed doors.
