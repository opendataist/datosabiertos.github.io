'Personally-owned devices are not public records,' Ottawa commissioner claims in FOIA denial

This article highlights significant tensions regarding transparency and public access to information within Ottawa County, directly impacting open data principles. The core issue centers on whether government business conducted on personal devices constitutes public records, challenging traditional definitions of public information. By denying access to instant messaging on personal phones, officials are effectively creating data silos that exclude citizens from understanding how tax dollars are spent, thereby undermining the open data ethos of comprehensive accessibility. Residents like Adrea Hill and Luke Sanner argue that these restrictions violate the spirit of the Freedom of Information Act. Their experiences reveal a pattern of delaying, denying, or charging high fees for simple requests, suggesting an institutional resistance to accountability. The argument that public officials are exempt from record retention requirements when using personal technology threatens the integrity of public records. This stance creates a loophole where crucial governance data remains hidden, preventing the community from holding leaders accountable for their decisions and expenditures. The relevance to open data lies in the evolving nature of digital governance. If personal devices become unchecked repositories of official business, the foundational promise of transparent, accessible public information is compromised. This case illustrates the urgent need to adapt open data frameworks to modern communication tools. Ensuring that all government-related communications are searchable and accessible, regardless of the device used, is essential for maintaining trust and ensuring that data remains a public resource rather than a guarded privilege.
