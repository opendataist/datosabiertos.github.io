The article argues that combating corruption is essential for restoring democratic trust in Latin America, particularly in Panama, where public confidence in institutions has reached critical lows. It posits that corruption is not merely a financial loss but a systemic threat that erodes the social fabric and weakens the rule of law, making proactive prevention strategies more vital than reactive sanctions. The core implication is that a comprehensive approach involving normative, institutional, and cultural changes is required to stabilize governance. Open data emerges as a pivotal tool in this preventive framework, enabling proactive citizen monitoring of public investment. By publishing granular, georeferenced data on public works and procurement, governments can facilitate transparency throughout the entire project lifecycle. This shift from opaque management to open, accessible information empowers society to hold officials accountable, directly addressing the information asymmetry that often facilitates illicit practices and waste in large-scale infrastructure projects. Furthermore, the text highlights the need to integrate transparency into political financing and civic education to sustain long-term integrity. It suggests that leveraging technology for social accountability, alongside fostering democratic values in schools, creates a resilient environment against corruption. This article is relevant to open data because it demonstrates how accessible information acts as a fundamental mechanism for social control, transforming passive citizens into active guardians of public resources and ensuring that digital transparency translates into tangible democratic trust.
