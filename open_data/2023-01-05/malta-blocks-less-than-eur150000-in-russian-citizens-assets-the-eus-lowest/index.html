Malta blocks less than €150,000 in Russian citizens’ assets, the EU’s lowest

Malta ranks last in the EU for freezing Russian assets, a situation complicated by its longstanding citizenship-by-investment program that has granted hundreds of passports to Russian nationals. This discrepancy highlights a significant enforcement gap, as Maltese authorities have seized minimal funds compared to other member states, despite the country hosting substantial wealth from sanctioned individuals. The European Commission has emphasized that asset freezing is mandatory, criticizing the uneven reporting and lack of urgency among states like Malta, which undermines collective efforts to penalize Russia’s war efforts. The case of Arkady Volozh illustrates the challenges of cross-border enforcement. Although the EU sanctioned the Yandex founder and seized his assets in Finland, Dutch courts allowed squatters to remain in his property, citing that renovations might increase asset value, which would be illegal under sanctions. This legal nuance shows how national interpretations of freezing orders can create loopholes, allowing sanctioned individuals to retain control or benefit from their holdings despite EU-wide restrictions. This article is relevant to open_data because it relies on publicly available EU statistics to expose inconsistencies in member states’ compliance. Transparent data publication is essential for identifying governance failures and holding jurisdictions accountable. By making seizure figures accessible, researchers and policymakers can pressure lagging nations to align with EU standards, demonstrating how open data serves as a critical tool for monitoring regulatory adherence and promoting integrity in international sanctions regimes.
