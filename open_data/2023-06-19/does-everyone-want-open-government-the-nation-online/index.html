Does everyone want open government? | The Nation Online

Does everyone want open government? | The Nation Online

Malawi’s launch of its second Open Government Partnership National Action Plan signals a renewed government commitment to transparency and accountability. By voluntarily adhering to international standards for fiscal transparency and public access to information, the state demonstrates a desire to reconfigure public institutions to align with open governance principles. This move is viewed as a genuine effort to manage public matters with proactive openness, treating transparency as a fundamental duty owed to citizens rather than a discretionary practice. However, the ambitious nature of the plan raises significant doubts regarding its feasibility within the planned timeframe. Critical commitments, such as requiring beneficial ownership information in public procurement and disclosing political party financing sources, appear disconnected from current institutional realities. These goals overlook existing gaps, such as the lack of established oversight bodies, making the projected outcomes seem overly idealistic and potentially unattainable given the complex social and political landscape. This article is relevant to open_data because it highlights the critical gap between data policy aspirations and practical implementation. It suggests that open government initiatives often assume universal willingness to share information, ignoring structural barriers and institutional deficiencies. For open_data advocates, the case serves as a cautionary example that without realistic baseline capacities and political will, ambitious transparency commitments may remain symbolic rather than transformative, ultimately hindering the establishment of functional, accessible data systems.
