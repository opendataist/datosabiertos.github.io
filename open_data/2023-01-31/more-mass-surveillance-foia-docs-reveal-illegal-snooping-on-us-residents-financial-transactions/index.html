More Mass Surveillance: FOIA Docs Reveal Illegal Snooping On US Residents’ Financial Transactions

The article highlights the severe erosion of digital privacy through the expansive and often illegal use of the Third Party Doctrine, which allows government agencies to obtain personal data without warrants by claiming users forfeit privacy when sharing information with businesses. This legal framework has been abused to facilitate mass surveillance, particularly targeting financial transactions. Law enforcement entities, including state and federal agencies, routinely use self-issued subpoenas to harvest vast amounts of private data from money transfer services, bypassing traditional judicial oversight and constitutional protections. This abuse is exemplified by a sprawling database in Arizona that aggregates hundreds of millions of money transfer records, accessible to thousands of law enforcement officers nationwide. The program disproportionately impacts marginalized communities and immigrants who rely on these services, demonstrating how systemic surveillance disproportionately affects those least able to resist government overreach. Despite previous rulings declaring such broad subpoenas illegal, authorities have continued to pressure companies into compliance, creating a centralized repository of financial data that operates largely outside legal boundaries. This issue is critically relevant to open data advocacy because it exposes the dark side of data availability: when private sector data can be freely accessed by government bodies without transparency or consent, it enables unchecked surveillance of vulnerable populations. It underscores the urgent need for legislative reform to overturn outdated legal doctrines and implement strict regulations on data sharing, ensuring that the availability of information does not come at the cost of fundamental civil liberties and privacy rights.
