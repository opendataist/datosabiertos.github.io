Commissioners Deny Request To Release Employee Survey Comments

Commissioners Deny Request To Release Employee Survey Comments

Livingston County officials have decided to keep employee survey feedback confidential, prioritizing the psychological safety of workers over public transparency. The Board of Commissioners upheld the denial of a Freedom of Information Act request, arguing that guaranteeing anonymity is essential for obtaining honest and constructive feedback. If employees fear their comments could be exposed, they are unlikely to communicate frank criticisms, which would render the survey’s insights meaningless and hinder organizational improvement. The controversy highlights a fundamental tension in open data practices: the balance between public accountability and internal trust. While proponents of full disclosure argue that taxpayer-funded information should be accessible, administrators contend that forced transparency creates a chilling effect on participation. They maintain that the survey’s value lies in its ability to drive internal policy changes, such as remote work adjustments, without subjecting staff to external political scrutiny or retaliation. This case is relevant to open data because it illustrates the limitations of applying rigid transparency laws to sensitive qualitative data. It suggests that not all public records serve the public interest when disclosure compromises the mechanism that generates the data. Understanding these nuances is crucial for developing frameworks that protect confidential feedback while still meeting democratic obligations for government accountability and effective service delivery.
