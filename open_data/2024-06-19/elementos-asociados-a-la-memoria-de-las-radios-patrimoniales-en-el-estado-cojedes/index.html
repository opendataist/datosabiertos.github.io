Elementos asociados a la memoria de las radios patrimoniales en el estado Cojedes

This article documents the cultural heritage associated with radio in Venezuela’s Cojedes state, highlighting a diverse array of tangible and intangible assets. It identifies preserved antique radio instruments across multiple municipalities and recognizes Cerro La Gloria as a strategic site for telecommunications infrastructure. Furthermore, it emphasizes the enduring influence of traditional music, such as Joropo, and the social institutions that sustain these cultural expressions through radio broadcasting. The narrative underscores the historical evolution of local media, beginning with the pioneering efforts of Ramón Isaac Carcaño in establishing Radio San Carlos in the 1950s. This early foundation catalyzed the development of technical expertise and subsequent media expansion in the region. The text chronicles how initial humble transmissions grew into a robust network that includes various FM stations and digital controls, transforming the local landscape from a modest service into a comprehensive telecommunications hub. Crucially, the article honors the “porters” of this heritage—numerous announcers, technicians, and cultural promoters who have passed down their skills and legacy across generations. These individuals are celebrated not just for their professional contributions but for their role in preserving regional identity and inspiring new audiences. Their collective efforts ensure that the memory of these radio stations remains a vital part of the community’s social fabric and cultural continuity. This documentation is highly relevant to open data, as it demonstrates how structured archival records can map and preserve intangible cultural heritage. By systematically cataloging specific entities, locations, and individual contributors, the article illustrates the potential of open data frameworks to safeguard historical narratives. Such transparency allows for broader public access to cultural resources, facilitating the protection of local identity and encouraging the reuse of heritage information by researchers, educators, and future generations.
