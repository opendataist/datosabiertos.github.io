FOIA Friday: Norfolk withholding juvenile victims’ records ‘runs afoul’ of FOIA law

FOIA Friday: Norfolk withholding juvenile victims’ records ‘runs afoul’ of FOIA law

The article highlights a persistent gap in Virginia between statutory transparency laws and actual governmental practice, where officials frequently treat records as private by default rather than public. Despite the Virginia Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) mandating openness, local leaders often resist access, requiring citizens to litigate for basic information. This cultural tendency undermines the spirit of open government and forces taxpayers to bear the legal costs of securing information that should be freely available. Recent appellate rulings demonstrate the critical importance of FOIA in holding authorities accountable for such secrecy. In Norfolk, courts clarified that juvenile victim protections do not extend to investigative files needed for legal defense, ordering the city to release records and reimburse legal fees. Similarly, in Warrenton, a blanket exemption for executive correspondence was rejected, affirming that data center planning documents must remain accessible. These decisions reinforce that legal exemptions are narrow and cannot be used as blanket tools to conceal routine administrative business. These cases are highly relevant to the open_data community as they underscore that legal rights to data are insufficient without active enforcement. They illustrate how opaque decision-making impacts civic engagement, particularly in controversial developments like data centers or airport contracts. For advocates of open data, this narrative serves as a stark reminder that technical access and legal frameworks must be accompanied by robust public vigilance to prevent local governments from operating in the shadows.
