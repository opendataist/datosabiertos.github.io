The Supreme Court rejects the appeals filed by the Government and the INE against the precautionary measures regarding overseas voting

The Supreme Court has upheld precautionary measures that suspend the voting rights of certain individuals naturalized under the Democratic Memory Law, rejecting the Government’s appeals. This judicial decision prioritizes administrative caution over electoral immediacy, maintaining restrictions in the Electoral Register of Absent Residents. By not lifting the suspensions before the elections, the High Court limits the exercise of suffrage for this specific group during the upcoming electoral process, despite the Government’s warnings about the logistical difficulties of verifying individual cases. The ruling establishes a strict documentary accreditation mechanism for those affected, who must individually demonstrate that they meet the requirements of descent and political exile to be excluded from the suspension. This requirement places a direct evidentiary burden on citizens, obliging them to obtain consular certifications to restore their voting rights. The resolution underscores the technical and legal complexity of managing large volumes of demographic and historical data within tight electoral timelines, highlighting the tension between legal certainty in census preparation and the accessibility of voting. This case is relevant to open data because it exposes the challenges of administrative interoperability and transparency in electoral data. It illustrates how the lack of standardization in verifying historical records and reliance on manual mechanisms can hinder the efficient management of public data. Furthermore, it raises questions about the governance of sensitive data and the capacity of digital systems to process complex legal exceptions, emphasizing the need to improve information flows between institutions to guarantee fundamental rights without compromising the integrity of the electoral process.
