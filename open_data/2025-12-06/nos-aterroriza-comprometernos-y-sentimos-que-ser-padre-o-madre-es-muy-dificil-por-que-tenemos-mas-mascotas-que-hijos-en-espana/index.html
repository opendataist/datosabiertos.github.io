“Nos aterroriza comprometernos y sentimos que ser padre o madre es muy difícil”: por qué tenemos más mascotas que hijos en España

“Nos aterroriza comprometernos y sentimos que ser padre o madre es muy difícil”: por qué tenemos más mascotas que hijos en España

The article concludes that the statistical predominance of pets over children in Spain does not indicate a societal preference for animals over human offspring, but rather reflects parallel, independent processes driven by socioeconomic constraints and evolving social dynamics. The decline in birth rates is primarily attributed to economic instability, housing difficulties, and the fear of parental commitment, which delay or prevent family expansion. Conversely, the rise in pet ownership addresses immediate emotional needs and offers a lower-commitment alternative for companionship, particularly among single individuals and the elderly. Regarding open data, this narrative highlights critical challenges in statistical accuracy and categorization. The absence of a unified registry for animals leads to significant discrepancies in census data, as many pets remain unchipped and deaths go unreported. This underscores the necessity for open data initiatives to enforce standardized, interoperable registries that distinguish between pet populations and human demographics, ensuring that public policy and research are based on precise, comparable metrics rather than flawed estimates. Furthermore, the shift in how animals are perceived—often humanized to fill emotional voids—demonstrates how qualitative social trends impact quantitative data collection. The "influencer" culture and the emotional projection onto pets complicate traditional household definitions, suggesting that open data frameworks must evolve to capture multidimensional aspects of modern living arrangements. Understanding these nuances is essential for creating inclusive data systems that reflect the true complexity of social structures rather than relying on outdated binaries.
