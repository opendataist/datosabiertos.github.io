Al mes, cinco hospitalizaciones por sobredosis del fentanilo en México

Al mes, cinco hospitalizaciones por sobredosis del fentanilo en México

The article highlights that while official hospital records show a low mortality rate among fentanyl-related admissions, these figures suffer from significant underreporting. By relying solely on data from platforms like Cubos Dinámicos, which tracks specific medical codes, the government overlooks critical interventions by paramedics and activists in public spaces. This gap prevents a comprehensive understanding of the crisis, as essential data on street-level treatments remains unrecorded and excluded from national health statistics. Focusing on states like Sinaloa and Baja California, the analysis reveals that fentanyl consumption is highly concentrated among homeless populations who face severe stigma and criminalization rather than receiving health care. The absence of national addiction surveys exacerbates this invisibility, allowing authorities to maintain a restrictive agenda that denies the existence of a widespread public health emergency. Consequently, vulnerable groups are subjected to social cleansing measures without any supportive reintegration policies, treating them as disposable rather than as individuals in need of medical attention. This situation is highly relevant to open data initiatives because it demonstrates the limitations of fragmented official datasets and the urgent need for inclusive, multi-source data collection. True open data in public health requires integrating information from civil society and emergency responders to capture the full scope of social realities. Without such transparency and the release of closed government agendas, policymakers cannot effectively address the root causes of drug-related crises or design evidence-based interventions that protect human rights.
