All major parties encashed electoral bonds; nobody has moral authority to say anything, says Finance Minister

The Supreme Court’s recent ruling invalidating the electoral bonds scheme highlights a critical gap in political finance transparency, directly impacting the open_data discourse. Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman defended the now-scrapped system by asserting its legal validity at the time of implementation, arguing that all major parties participated. However, the court’s decision underscores that opaque funding mechanisms violate citizens' rights to information, reinforcing the necessity for publicly accessible data in democratic processes. The minister acknowledged that the current return to previous funding standards is inferior and called for a reformed system. This admission signals a potential shift toward greater accountability, yet it also reveals the complexity of balancing donor privacy with public interest. For open_data advocates, this moment emphasizes the urgent need for standardized, machine-readable disclosure formats that allow independent verification of political contributions, ensuring that transparency is not merely symbolic but functional. Rebuttals regarding law enforcement motives and internal party dynamics serve as distractions from the core issue: the lack of verifiable data on political financing. By dismissing allegations of political vendetta, the government attempts to isolate electoral funding from broader governance criticisms. Ultimately, the debate reinforces why open data standards are essential for democratic integrity, enabling citizens to trace influence and hold power to account through accessible, reliable information rather than opaque legalistic defenses.
