The official delineation of metropolitan areas in Mexico has undergone significant restructuring, drastically reducing the number of recognized zones by redefining their population criteria and categories. This methodological change, driven by institutions such as INEGI and CONAPO, aims to more accurately reflect the demographic and urban reality, distinguishing municipal metropolises from conurbated zones under stricter population thresholds. The new classification implies that many previously designated areas have been reclassified, altering the traditional perspective on the country's urban density and structure. The implications of this new cartography are profound for public management and resource allocation. Lawmakers emphasize that metropolitan areas house the majority of the national population and face common crises, such as water scarcity and pollution, which require specific legal frameworks and policies. The numerical reduction does not signify a decrease in these problems but rather a need to adapt regulatory mechanisms and infrastructure to effectively serve millions of inhabitants in complex urbanized contexts. This information is crucial for the open data movement, as government institutions have integrated these new delineations into public consultation platforms, such as INEGI's census system. The availability of disaggregated data by metropolitan area enables citizens, researchers, and civil society organizations to analyze territorial inequalities, monitor service indicators, and demand transparency. Thus, urban planning is transformed into an evidence-based process, fostering more inclusive and responsive governance that addresses the real needs of metropolitan areas.
