The article highlights the implementation of the European Travel Information and Authorisation System (ETIAS), a border security mechanism that will require citizens of third countries, such as Britons post-Brexit, to obtain prior authorization before entering the Schengen Area. This measure reflects the post-referendum geopolitical reality, introducing an additional layer of bureaucracy that increases administrative complexity for tourists and creates practical obstacles, particularly for those traveling with families, thereby generating frustration over the loss of previously existing freedom of movement. Despite criticisms regarding the administrative burden and associated costs, the analysis suggests that these restrictions will not significantly deter tourist flows. On the contrary, it is argued that ETIAS could enhance the region’s perception of security and stability, thereby strengthening travelers’ confidence. The travel expert concludes that, although it represents a political and practical price to pay for border sovereignty, the system is understandable and designed to protect borders without disrupting the existing economic and cultural ties between the United Kingdom and Europe. This information is relevant to the field of open data because it illustrates the tension between the interoperability of border management systems and the end-user experience. ETIAS relies on the processing and cross-referencing of personal data for security purposes, raising critical issues concerning algorithmic transparency, privacy, and how governments disclose or conceal risk-assessment criteria. Furthermore, it serves as a case study on how new digital policies affect human mobility, underscoring the need for travel restriction data to be accessible, clear, and readily available to citizens.
