Monetary poverty in Mexico fell from 43.9 million to 26.6 million people during the administrations of López Obrador and Sheinbaum, according to the World Bank, which attributes the decline to higher labor incomes and social programs.

Monetary poverty in Mexico fell from 43.9 million to 26.6 million people during the administrations of López Obrador and Sheinbaum, according to the World Bank, which attributes the decline to higher labor incomes and social programs.

The World Bank projects a significant reduction in monetary poverty in Mexico, estimating a considerable decline in the affected population through 2026. This optimism is grounded in the rise in real labor incomes and the expansion of social programs, factors that have also contributed to lower income inequality during the analyzed period. It is crucial to distinguish these figures from INEGI’s official measurements, as they employ different methodologies. While the World Bank’s report relies strictly on daily income thresholds, the national measurement incorporates multidimensional social deprivations. This technical difference means that the estimates are not directly comparable, underscoring the need to understand the specific criteria underlying each indicator to avoid misinterpretations about the population’s actual well-being. The relevance of this article for open data lies in the methodological transparency required when comparing heterogeneous datasets. It illustrates how different sources can yield divergent results for the same phenomenon, highlighting the importance of understanding the underlying assumptions and definitions. To properly analyze socioeconomic evolution, it is essential to integrate and contextualize these complementary variables rather than treating them as equivalent absolute numbers.
