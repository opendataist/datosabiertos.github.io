Adobe Firefly was supposed to be the safe AI option – so what's changed?

Adobe Firefly was supposed to be the safe AI option – so what's changed?

Adobe’s introduction of third-party AI models alongside its native Firefly service significantly complicates the promise of "commercially safe" outputs. While Firefly was previously distinguished by its use of licensed and public-domain data, the platform now aggregates models from various providers with different training histories and terms. This shift suggests that the safety guarantee no longer applies universally, requiring users to scrutinize individual models rather than relying on a blanket Adobe endorsement. The article highlights a critical disconnect between marketing perceptions and legal realities, noting that licensed training data does not guarantee that generated images won’t resemble existing artists' styles. By mixing partner models with its own, Adobe forces creators and agencies to assume the burden of verifying the ethical sourcing and licensing conditions of each specific tool. This places the responsibility for ensuring commercial safety squarely on the user, who may lack the time or expertise to investigate the provenance of every integrated model. This development is highly relevant to open data advocates because it underscores the necessity of transparency in AI training datasets. The situation demonstrates how opaque training methods can obscure potential copyright infringement and ethical violations, even within platforms that claim responsible practices. For the open data community, this serves as a cautionary tale: without clear, accessible, and specific disclosure of data origins, users cannot effectively assess the legitimacy of AI-generated content, potentially eroding trust in digital creative tools.
