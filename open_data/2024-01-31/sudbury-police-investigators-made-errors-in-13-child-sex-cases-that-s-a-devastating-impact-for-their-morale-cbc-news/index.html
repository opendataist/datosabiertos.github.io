Sudbury police investigators made errors in 13 child sex cases: 'That's a devastating impact for their morale' | CBC News

A CBC investigation revealed that systemic errors by the Greater Sudbury Police Service compromised thirteen child pornography cases, including one where charges were dropped due to investigators soliciting passwords from suspects before they could consult legal counsel. This practice violated constitutional rights, highlighting a pattern of procedural failures that undermined the integrity of these high-stakes investigations and resulted in significant delays in court appearances and disclosure to defense teams. The incident underscores the critical importance of transparency and accountability in law enforcement, particularly when handling sensitive digital evidence. Although police leadership characterized the mistakes as unintentional and attributed them to evolving case law, defense attorneys argued that the widespread nature of the errors indicated a deeper failure to adhere to constitutional protections. This conflict highlights the tension between investigative efficiency and the strict adherence to due process, which is essential for maintaining public confidence in the justice system. This story is highly relevant to open data because it demonstrates how freedom of information requests can expose institutional flaws that internal reviews might otherwise obscure. By challenging initial refusals to release documents, investigators proved that public scrutiny outweighs privacy concerns in cases involving police conduct. The eventual release of these records illustrates the power of open data in fostering accountability, allowing civil society to verify claims of systemic issues and ensuring that police services are held to high standards of transparency and professional integrity.
