The study reveals that Christians are significantly more inclined to integrate artificial intelligence into their professional routines compared to their non-Christian counterparts. This disparity suggests that religious affiliation correlates with a higher comfort level in adopting emerging technologies for productivity, challenging assumptions that faith-based communities might inherently resist modern digital tools in the workplace. Despite this adoption, there is a clear boundary in how AI is perceived regarding spiritual matters. Most individuals, regardless of their religious stance, view the technology as suitable for research and answering general questions but largely distrust its ability to provide reliable advice or deepen religious understanding. Trust in AI for interpreting Christian teachings remains low, with a majority of Americans emphasizing the need for caution when handling nuanced ethical or theological issues. This article is highly relevant to open data as it highlights significant demographic biases in technology usage and trust. It underscores the importance of analyzing user behavior across diverse cultural and religious groups when developing ethical AI frameworks. Furthermore, it demonstrates that open data initiatives must account for varying levels of digital literacy and skepticism to ensure inclusive and accurate representation of how different communities interact with data-driven tools.
