What Internet Archive’s failed appeal means to free access to digital materials? | Explained

What Internet Archive’s failed appeal means to free access to digital materials? | Explained

The U.S. Second Circuit Court of Appeals rejected the Internet Archive’s defense of controlled digital lending, ruling that scanning and distributing copyrighted books without permission violates copyright law. The court determined that creating full digital copies for free access is not "transformative" under fair use doctrine. Consequently, the Archive must remove hundreds of thousands of titles, affirming authors’ rights to compensation and protecting the incentive to create new works. This decision highlights a critical tension between preserving public access to information and enforcing traditional intellectual property rights. While the court acknowledged that licensing fees burden libraries, it prioritized the legal necessity of compensating creators. The ruling distinguishes this digital lending model from traditional library practices, establishing a significant precedent that limits how institutions can digitize and share copyrighted materials for public benefit without explicit authorization. For open data and digital preservation advocates, this case is a pivotal warning about the legal fragility of mass digitization efforts. It underscores the risk that open repositories may face severe restrictions when distributing modern copyrighted content, potentially limiting historical preservation and equitable access to knowledge. The contrast with AI training cases further complicates the landscape, suggesting that the definition of fair use remains unsettled and highly context-dependent, which threatens the sustainability of open archives globally.
