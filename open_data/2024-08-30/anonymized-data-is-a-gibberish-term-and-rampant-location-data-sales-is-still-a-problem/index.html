'Anonymized Data' Is A Gibberish Term, And Rampant Location Data Sales Is Still A Problem

The article argues that the widespread reliance on "anonymization" to justify the collection and sale of personal data is fundamentally flawed. It highlights that removing direct identifiers does not prevent re-identification, as diverse datasets can be cross-referenced to pinpoint specific individuals. This deception allows corporations and government officials to claim compliance with privacy standards while continuing practices that leave users vulnerable to surveillance and misuse. Location data is identified as a critical risk, as it reveals intimate details about a person’s life, including their home, workplace, and daily habits. Despite the clear potential for abuse by stalkers, law enforcement, and commercial brokers, the industry operates with significant opacity. Companies often refuse to disclose how they acquire this data or which apps contribute to it, relying on the illusion of anonymity to maintain public trust and avoid scrutiny while profiting from the trade. This lack of regulation persists due to effective industry lobbying that has stalled meaningful privacy reform and federal legislation. Consequently, the status quo continues, with companies maintaining that oversight is unnecessary while scandals recur. This situation is vital to the open data community because it exposes the gap between open access to datasets and the ethical implications of using them. It underscores the need for stricter transparency and accountability when releasing or utilizing data, reminding us that open data practices must prioritize user safety and recognize that technical anonymization is insufficient without robust governance.
