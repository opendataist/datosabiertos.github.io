From UK Crime to Mexico Ads: Why Open Data Needs Better Transparency

We often talk about the world we want to leave for our children. We want it to be cleaner, safer, and more honest. This honesty comes from data. Good data is raw, easy to find, and ready for everyone to see. It should not be hidden behind paywalls or confusing government words. Two stories show why open data is powerful and why we need to improve it.

Think about safety in East Cambridgeshire. Crime data tells a story that big statistics cannot. National news focuses on broad trends. But raw data shows that crime in this area is spread out. Most incidents are minor. Violent acts and bad behavior make up more than half of all cases. Different places have different risks based on how the land is used. For example, shoplifting happens mostly in shopping areas.

Open data helps people turn these free datasets into useful insights. Users can use public tools like Police.UK. This lets them look at small details on the street. They do not have to rely on secret reports or vague summaries. This detailed look reveals hidden patterns in community safety. It helps leaders use resources better without buying expensive, closed-source information. Access to data helps people understand their communities on a human level. Our guide on how to use an open-data portal’s API: CKAN, DCAT and OpenDataSoft shows you the practical steps to get started.

However, transparency has challenges. In Nuevo León, Mexico, the state government spent millions on Meta ads. Governor Samuel García spent more than half of that money. Meta’s transparency portal shows total numbers. But it does not say if the money came from public taxes or private sources. This lack of detail makes it hard for citizens to tell the difference. They cannot easily see if the ads were for official government work or for the Governor’s personal political brand.

This case shows a big problem in the open data movement. Making government spending visible helps hold leaders accountable. But current rules often fall short. The data is not detailed enough, and funding sources are unclear. Users cannot hold power accountable if the data is incomplete or misleading. True openness needs data that is detailed enough to tell the whole truth. Laws and licenses must change to ensure that open data is truly useful and clear. Our guide on what licence open data carries, and what you may do with it provides essential context for those looking to understand the legal boundaries of using public information.

These two stories, one from the UK and one from Mexico, show that open data is a tool for justice and clarity. People who analyze crime data to keep their children safe share the same goal as those who check political spending. They both want a transparent and shared world. By asking for better data and learning how to use it, we build that better world, one dataset at a time.
