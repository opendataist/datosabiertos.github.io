The Internet Archive’s Open Library initiative faces imminent legal defeat in its copyright infringement lawsuit because its Controlled Digital Lending model fundamentally misunderstands the first sale doctrine. Unlike physical books, digital ebooks are typically licensed rather than sold, meaning the doctrine that allows libraries to lend physical copies does not extend to creating and distributing unauthorized digital duplicates. The court is expected to rule that IA’s practice constitutes market substitution rather than fair use, as it bypasses the specific licensing agreements publishers have established for digital content. This distinction is crucial for the open data and digital preservation communities, as it clarifies that expanding access does not justify bypassing copyright permissions when a commercial market exists. The ruling will likely prevent the erosion of publishers' exclusive rights to control digital distribution, setting a precedent that digital lending cannot simply mirror physical lending mechanisms without permission. This outcome protects the economic incentives necessary for content creation while forcing digital libraries to negotiate proper access rights. The decision underscores the importance of respecting legal frameworks that balance public access with creator rights in the digital age. For open data advocates, it highlights the necessity of engaging with copyright holders rather than circumventing them to achieve universal access goals. By rejecting the notion that digitization for free distribution is automatically transformative or harmless, the case reinforces that sustainable open access must operate within, not against, existing intellectual property laws.
