Ecuador: Dividendos y utilidades no distribuidas con nuevos tributos desde septiembre

Ecuador: Dividendos y utilidades no distribuidas con nuevos tributos desde septiembre

The recently approved Transparency Law introduces significant tax reforms for foundations and NGOs, aiming to simplify income tax obligations and promote the reinvestment of profits while enhancing fiscal control. By implementing a unified tax rate on dividends and establishing progressive contributions on undistributed earnings, the legislation seeks to encourage capital circulation and job creation. However, experts argue these measures may disincentivize internal reinvestment by penalizing retained profits, shifting the focus toward immediate distribution to shareholders rather than long-term corporate growth. The regulatory changes also include controversial provisions regarding social security liabilities, specifically exempting state institutions from paying debts incurred during their liquidation process. Critics view this as an unconstitutional precedent that allows the government to default on its obligations while simultaneously penalizing the private sector. This creates a tension between the law’s stated goal of transparency and its practical application, which appears to prioritize fiscal relief for public entities over the financial integrity of social security funds. This article is relevant to open data because it highlights the critical link between regulatory transparency and data accessibility. For the law to effectively combat corruption and ensure accountability, the tax obligations and financial flows of foundations and NGOs must be publicly accessible and easily auditable. Without robust open data frameworks, the promised simplification and control mechanisms remain theoretical, undermining the law’s potential to foster genuine social transparency and trust in public institutions.
