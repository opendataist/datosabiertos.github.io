A Proposal for a UN Freedom of Information Act Never Got Off the Ground

The United Nations is portrayed as an institution that vigorously advocates for global press freedom while simultaneously maintaining profound internal opacity. Despite being a key promoter of human rights, the organization lacks basic transparency, with diplomats and senior officials routinely refusing on-the-record comments or ignoring media inquiries. This contradiction highlights a significant gap between the UN’s external advocacy for open societies and its internal culture of secrecy, where accountability to the public is often sacrificed for bureaucratic comfort. Central to this issue is the failure to establish a UN Freedom of Information Act, a proposal that has stagnated due to inaction by member states and resistance within the Secretariat. Experts argue that without such legal frameworks, the UN cannot claim to support effective and inclusive institutions, as it lacks the transparency required to lead by example. The absence of a mechanism for citizens and journalists to access information undermines the organization’s legitimacy and hinders efforts to hold powerful bodies accountable for their actions and decisions. This article is critical to open data discussions because it underscores the necessity of structural transparency in international governance. For open data initiatives to succeed at a global level, organizations must move beyond voluntary disclosure to mandated, legally enforceable access to information. The UN’s reluctance mirrors broader challenges in the data ecosystem, demonstrating that without strong legal protections for data access, even well-intentioned transparency efforts are likely to fail, leaving the public in the dark about how global policies are formed.
