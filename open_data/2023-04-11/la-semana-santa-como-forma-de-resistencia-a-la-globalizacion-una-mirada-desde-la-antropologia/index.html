La Semana Santa como forma de resistencia a la globalización: una mirada desde la antropología

Seville’s Holy Week transcends its religious origins to function as a profound symbol of local identity and springtime renewal. Rather than merely a liturgical observance, it serves as a cultural anchor that fosters a deep sense of belonging and resistance against the homogenizing forces of globalization. This phenomenon allows communities to reaffirm their distinct identities, contrasting sharply with the transient nature of modern, consumerist lifestyles. The celebration acts as a "total social fact," integrating diverse emotional, aesthetic, and artistic dimensions that cannot be reduced to pure theology. It humanizes the sacred through familiar, personalized imagery, enabling participants to engage with figures of suffering and maternal protection in a horizontal, familial manner. This approach democratizes the experience, allowing people across the political spectrum, including those with left-wing or secular views, to find meaning in the shared cultural heritage rather than solely in dogmatic faith. This article is relevant to open data because it challenges the reductionist interpretation of complex cultural phenomena through singular metrics. It illustrates that while religious institutions may attempt to monopolize the narrative, the true essence of the event lies in its multifaceted, community-driven meanings. Understanding such traditions requires moving beyond binary or purely statistical classifications to capture the rich, overlapping social layers that define collective identity, highlighting the importance of qualitative context in interpreting societal data.
