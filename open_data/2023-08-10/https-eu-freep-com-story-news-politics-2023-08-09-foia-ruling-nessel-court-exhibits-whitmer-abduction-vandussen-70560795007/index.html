A Michigan court has ruled that the Attorney General’s office must release criminal trial exhibits that were introduced in open court, rejecting claims that these documents are exempt from public records laws. The judge determined that once evidence is presented publicly during legal proceedings, it loses its status as confidential investigative material. This decision clarifies that transparency obligations override general concerns about protecting ongoing investigations or juror impartiality when the documents have already been exposed to public scrutiny in a courtroom setting. This ruling is significant for the open data community because it establishes a clear precedent for the accessibility of judicial records. It reinforces the principle that government-held data, particularly in legal contexts, should remain available to the public unless specific, proven harms justify secrecy. By dismissing broad exemptions related to privacy and fair trial rights, the court affirms that citizens have a right to access the factual basis of public legal actions, ensuring that government operations remain observable and accountable. The outcome highlights the ongoing tension between institutional secrecy and the public’s right to know. While the Attorney General initially resisted disclosure, fearing potential impacts on future trials, the court found these arguments unsubstantiated. This case underscores the importance of active advocacy by journalists and citizens in enforcing freedom of information laws, demonstrating that legal challenges can successfully compel the release of withheld government data and set stronger standards for digital and document transparency.
