NT bill attempts to shield backbenchers from Freedom of Information laws

NT bill attempts to shield backbenchers from Freedom of Information laws

The Northern Territory government is proposing legislation to significantly restrict public access to internal correspondence by exempting assistant ministers and their staff from Freedom of Information requests. This measure effectively categorizes such communications as non-government information, thereby shielding the expanded team of political aides from transparency obligations that apply to traditional public sector bodies. The move raises serious concerns about democratic accountability, as it reduces the mechanisms available to journalists and citizens for scrutinizing government decision-making. This legislative change is a direct response to legal challenges initiated by advocacy groups, particularly regarding environmental and mining regulations. The government argues that resisting these requests has imposed excessive financial burdens on taxpayers through prolonged legal battles. By codifying this exemption, officials aim to prevent future lawsuits and streamline administrative processes, framing the change as a necessary defense against what they describe as frivolous litigation rather than a genuine effort to reduce public oversight. This development is critically relevant to open data advocacy because it represents a regression in institutional transparency, mirroring similar restrictive trends observed at the federal level. By narrowing the scope of what constitutes accessible public information, such laws undermine the fundamental right to know and erode the checks and balances essential for a functioning democracy. For open data proponents, this highlights the urgent need to protect existing access frameworks and resist efforts that prioritize political convenience over public accountability and the free flow of information.
