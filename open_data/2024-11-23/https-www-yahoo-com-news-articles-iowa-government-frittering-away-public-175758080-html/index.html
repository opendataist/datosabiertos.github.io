This article highlights a concerning trend where local government officials circumvent public scrutiny by holding private, informal gatherings to discuss controversial issues before formal meetings. By exploiting technicalities in open meetings laws, such as avoiding quorums, officials prioritize their convenience over transparency, effectively excluding citizens from the crucial deliberative process. This behavior undermines the legislative intent that ambiguity in access should always favor openness, replacing democratic debate with secretive consensus-building. The relevance to open data and civic engagement is profound, as transparency is the foundation of public trust. When governments operate in the shadows, they erode citizen confidence and deny taxpayers the ability to understand the rationale behind decisions that affect their lives. This lack of openness creates an information asymmetry that favors officials and discourages active civic participation, suggesting that mere availability of data is insufficient if the decision-making processes themselves are concealed. Ultimately, the article argues that intentional secrecy in local governance poses a significant risk to democratic integrity. It calls for stricter adherence to open meetings principles and potentially more explicit legal statutes to prevent officials from hiding deliberations. Ensuring that government belongs to the people, rather than the officials, requires a commitment to visible, accessible, and inclusive decision-making processes that allow the public to monitor and understand their local leaders' actions.
