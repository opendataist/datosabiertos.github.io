Amicus Briefs Filed in Internet Archive Copyright Case

The appeal in the Internet Archive’s copyright case highlights the critical tension between traditional publishing rights and the digital evolution of library services. Publishers argue that extending the "first sale" doctrine to digital loans undermines the economic incentives for creators and disrupts established markets. They contend that allowing mass digital lending without permission sets a dangerous precedent that could destabilize industries relying on copyright protections to sustain content creation. Conversely, the Internet Archive and its supporters maintain that Controlled Digital Lending is a legitimate fair use essential for preserving cultural heritage and ensuring equitable access to information. Librarians and advocates emphasize that libraries serve a vital public mission, and restricting their ability to lend legally acquired physical books in digital format infringes on reader privacy and educational goals. They argue that the current legal interpretation disproportionately harms nonprofit institutions and restricts the democratic function of libraries. This case is profoundly relevant to the open data and open access communities because it determines the future legal viability of digitizing public resources. A ruling against the Internet Archive could establish a restrictive framework for how digital copies of physical materials are shared, potentially limiting the availability of scholarly and cultural data. Conversely, a victory for the Archive would reinforce the principle that digital preservation and distribution for nonprofit educational purposes should remain protected, thereby supporting broader initiatives aimed at democratizing knowledge and maintaining open repositories.
