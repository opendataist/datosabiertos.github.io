Reports indicate that the builder of the Mountain Valley Pipeline claimed to have notified federal regulators of a pressure test rupture. However, freedom of information requests reveal no direct correspondence with the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission in the days following the incident, despite the agency’s mandate to safeguard the public. The builder instead relied on routine construction and environmental compliance documents that omitted the urgency of the rupture, describing only water discharge and sediment issues rather than the pipe failure itself. The incident ultimately became public knowledge through concerned landowners who observed environmental damage and photographed the damaged infrastructure, leading to state-level investigations. This discrepancy between official regulatory notification and public discovery raises significant concerns about transparency and accountability in critical energy infrastructure projects. It suggests that safety breaches may be downplayed in official records, potentially hindering effective oversight by federal bodies responsible for approving such massive undertakings. This case is highly relevant to open data initiatives, as it highlights the necessity of accessible, transparent public records in holding corporations and regulators accountable. When critical safety information is buried in routine reports or withheld from immediate public view, citizens cannot effectively monitor environmental impacts or regulatory compliance. Strengthening open data policies ensures that stakeholders can scrutinize infrastructure projects in real time, preventing the obfuscation of safety incidents and promoting greater trust in federal oversight mechanisms.
