Sierra Leone’s Freedom of Information Act needs activating

The enactment of Sierra Leone’s Freedom of Information Law represents a critical legislative milestone for transparency, yet its true value remains unrealized without effective implementation. The central issue is not the law's existence, but the government’s failure to establish the necessary administrative structures, funding, and enforcement mechanisms immediately following its passage. Without these foundational elements, the legislation risks becoming a symbolic gesture rather than a functional tool for accountability, leaving citizens unable to exercise their right to access government records. A significant concern is the potential redundancy between the proposed Freedom of Information Commission and the existing Open Government Initiative. Both entities share similar goals regarding transparency and public participation, leading to fears that maintaining both would waste limited resources and create bureaucratic confusion. To ensure sustainability and efficiency, stakeholders argue that a unified approach is necessary, prioritizing the establishment of a strong, independent commission with enforcement powers over parallel, duplicative agencies that dilute impact and strain public funds. This situation is vital to the open data movement as it highlights that legal frameworks alone are insufficient; they must be accompanied by robust institutional support and political will. For open data to thrive, governments must proactively build the systems that allow data to be accessible, usable, and accountable. The Sierra Leone case serves as a cautionary tale that without deliberate investment in implementation infrastructure, even well-intentioned laws can fail to empower citizens or foster genuine governmental openness.
