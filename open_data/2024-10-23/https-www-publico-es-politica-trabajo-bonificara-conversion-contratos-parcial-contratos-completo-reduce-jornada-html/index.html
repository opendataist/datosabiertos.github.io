The article outlines ongoing negotiations to reduce the standard weekly working hours to 37.5, highlighting the government’s strategy to facilitate this transition through targeted incentives. A central element is the “Plan PYME 375,” which aims to support small businesses by offering social security bonuses for hiring permanent staff and converting part-time contracts into full-time roles to cover the resulting labor gaps. These measures are designed to stimulate job creation and improve employment quality, particularly in sectors such as commerce and hospitality, without imposing excessive financial burdens on the labor market. Simultaneously, the government is strengthening compliance mechanisms by developing an interoperable time-tracking system and proposing stricter penalties for non-compliance. The focus has shifted from penalizing companies as a whole to imposing sanctions per employee, with increased severity for violations that endanger workers’ health. This approach underscores a commitment to enforcing legal standards and combating excessive overtime, ensuring that the reduction in working hours translates into tangible improvements in working conditions and well-being. The relevance to open data lies in the implementation of a unified, interoperable time-tracking register. Such a system requires standardized data formats and transparent reporting mechanisms to effectively monitor compliance and detect fraud. This initiative aligns with open data principles by promoting the collection, sharing, and analysis of labor market data. It enhances accountability and allows for better public scrutiny of working hours, ultimately supporting data-driven policymaking and the protection of workers’ rights through greater transparency.
