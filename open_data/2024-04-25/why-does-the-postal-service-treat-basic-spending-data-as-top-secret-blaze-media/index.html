Why does the Postal Service treat basic spending data as top secret? | Blaze Media

The article highlights a systemic failure in government transparency, where the process for accessing public information has become increasingly burdensome and inefficient. A significant backlog in Freedom of Information Act requests demonstrates that agencies are prioritizing secrecy over accountability, making it difficult for citizens to understand how taxpayer money is spent. This obstruction undermines the democratic principle that citizens have a right to know how their government operates, turning what should be a routine access mechanism into a cumbersome bureaucratic hurdle. Specific agencies, particularly those involved in defense and public services, are exacerbating this crisis by failing to process requests within statutory timeframes and issuing disproportionate denial rates. The United States Postal Service serves as a prime example, rejecting the vast majority of requests and using commercial exemptions to withhold basic financial data. By treating basic procurement costs as trade secrets, these entities obscure critical fiscal details from the public, effectively hiding the true cost of government operations behind a veil of administrative secrecy and vague policy interpretations. This lack of openness is critically relevant to open data advocates because it reveals the gap between legal frameworks and actual data accessibility. Even when data exists, aggressive redactions and procedural delays prevent its meaningful use, rendering open data initiatives ineffective if the underlying mechanisms for release are broken. To achieve true transparency, policymakers must enforce concrete timelines for request processing and reject overly broad exemptions, ensuring that public funds are not accompanied by public opacity.
