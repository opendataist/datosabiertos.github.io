Royal family's 'dark' exemption to FOI requests must end, say campaigners

The article highlights a significant barrier to transparency within the UK government, where specific exemptions shield royal correspondence from Freedom of Information requests. This legal framework allows documents concerning the monarchy to remain classified for over a century, effectively preventing historians and the public from accessing records that might shed light on the private and professional conduct of senior royals. The central implication is a stark contradiction between the monarchy’s claim to be public servants and their exemption from public scrutiny. Critics argue that because the institution is funded by taxpayers and plays a role in national governance, it should adhere to the same accountability standards as other government bodies. The ability of the state to withhold information without justification suggests a culture of secrecy that undermines democratic principles and the nation’s reputation. This issue is critically relevant to open data advocates because it exposes how legal frameworks can institutionalize opacity. The case of Prince Andrew illustrates that even when records likely exist, bureaucratic exemptions can block access, demonstrating that technical availability of data is insufficient without robust legal rights to disclosure. It serves as a reminder that true openness requires removing arbitrary barriers that protect certain entities from oversight.
