'Anonymized Data' Is A Gibberish Term, And Rampant Location Data Sales Is Still A Problem

The article challenges the pervasive myth that data anonymization effectively protects user privacy. It highlights that location data, often stripped of direct identifiers, remains highly re-identifiable when cross-referenced with other datasets. Experts demonstrate that simple movement patterns reveal intimate details about a person’s life, making the claim that such data is anonymous fundamentally false and dangerous. This lack of genuine anonymity fuels a opaque industry where brokers buy, sell, and trade personal location records with minimal transparency or user consent. Companies like Veraset operate in secrecy to avoid public backlash, masking the sheer volume of data collected from apps and wireless carriers. Despite high-profile scandals and confirmed abuses by stalkers and law enforcement, regulatory efforts and legislative reforms have stalled due to intense corporate lobbying, leaving users vulnerable to continuous exploitation. This situation is critically relevant to open_data because it exposes the flaws in how public and private sectors handle supposedly “anonymous” open datasets. When governments procure location data under the guise of anonymity without rigorous de-identification standards, they risk enabling surveillance and privacy violations. It underscores the urgent need for strict open data governance that prioritizes true privacy protection over the illusion of anonymity, ensuring that open data initiatives do not inadvertently facilitate the same abuses seen in the private sector.
