La Nación / Policía niega espionaje y afirma que se usaron solo datos abiertos

The article highlights the tension between state security protocols and civil liberties during recent protests in Asunción. Police leadership denies allegations of arbitrary detention and illegal surveillance, asserting that their actions strictly adhered to constitutional mandates. They argue that arrests were necessary responses to specific threats of violence and vandalism, emphasizing that the information used was derived from openly accessible public data rather than intercepted private communications. This distinction regarding data access is crucial for open data discourse, as it challenges how law enforcement interacts with public digital footprints. By claiming that group chat invitations and visible preparatory messages constitute "open data," authorities attempt to legitimize their use in intelligence gathering. This narrative forces a critical examination of the boundary between public information available to anyone and private conversations, raising questions about the scope of legitimate surveillance when individuals voluntarily join open digital communities. Ultimately, the incident underscores the legal complexities of policing public assemblies in a digital age. While investigations continue despite the release of detainees, the debate centers on whether the use of visible, non-encrypted public data violates privacy rights. This case serves as a significant reference point for defining how open information can be responsibly utilized by state institutions without compromising individual rights or eroding public trust in democratic institutions.
