‘Disregard for transparency’: Utah Legislature’s public records laws earn it a ‘Black Hole’ award

‘Disregard for transparency’: Utah Legislature’s public records laws earn it a ‘Black Hole’ award

The Utah Legislature received the Society of Professional Journalists’ Black Hole Award for enacting legislation that significantly restricts public access to government records. This recognition highlights a systemic shift toward reduced transparency, where legislative actions are designed to shield information rather than expose it to public scrutiny. By prioritizing administrative efficiency and political convenience over open government principles, the state has undermined the fundamental right of citizens to hold their leaders accountable. Central to this criticism is the replacement of the diverse State Records Committee with a single governor-appointed attorney, which consolidates power and diminishes independent oversight. Simultaneously, new restrictions make it financially prohibitive for ordinary citizens to challenge denied records in court by requiring proof of bad faith to recover legal fees. These changes effectively silence those without substantial resources, creating a chilling effect on the pursuit of public information and ensuring that only well-funded entities can navigate the appeals process. This situation is highly relevant to open data because it demonstrates how legal and administrative frameworks can actively obstruct data availability. When governments make it difficult to request, review, or appeal the release of records, they directly hinder the flow of open data necessary for journalism, research, and civic engagement. The Utah case serves as a cautionary example of how policy decisions can erode transparency infrastructure, emphasizing the need for robust protections that ensure data remains accessible and subject to public oversight.
