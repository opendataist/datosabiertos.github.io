‘Dubious’ use of the Freedom of Information act stopping access to files on Prince Andrew, researchers say

‘Dubious’ use of the Freedom of Information act stopping access to files on Prince Andrew, researchers say

This article highlights a concerning trend of increasing opacity regarding government records on the British royal family, specifically focusing on Prince Andrew. Researchers and authors are facing significant barriers when attempting to access files through Freedom of Information requests, often encountering contradictory responses or arbitrary restrictions that delay public release for decades. This lack of transparency raises serious questions about whether the government is actively concealing information related to the Duke of York, particularly amid allegations involving his conduct and associations. The situation is further complicated by the National Archives’ recent decision to reclose documents that were previously available for public inspection. Academics argue that this retrospective application of exemptions undermines the intent of open data legislation, as internal reviews lack external scrutiny or judicial oversight. Consequently, files on historical matters, including royal travel and military training, are being hidden under vague national security or privacy justifications, effectively reversing years of progress toward greater governmental accountability and open access. This case is highly relevant to open data principles because it illustrates how legal frameworks can be manipulated to restrict public access to information. The arbitrary nature of these closures and the inconsistency in handling requests demonstrate a systemic failure to uphold the spirit of transparency. For open data advocates, this serves as a critical example of how institutional power can be used to withhold records, suggesting that without stricter enforcement and external validation, the promise of accessible public information remains fragile and easily compromised.
