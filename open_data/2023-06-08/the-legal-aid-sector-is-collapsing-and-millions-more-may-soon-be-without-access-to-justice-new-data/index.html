The legal aid sector is collapsing and millions more may soon be without access to justice – new data

The UK government recently raised legal aid eligibility thresholds to account for inflation, theoretically granting access to over two million additional people. However, eligibility does not equate to actual access. The sector is experiencing a severe collapse in provision, characterized by a dramatic reduction in the number of active providers across critical areas such as housing, mental health, and asylum. Despite more people qualifying for assistance, the infrastructure required to deliver it is rapidly disappearing, leaving many without viable legal support. This crisis stems from years of austerity and stagnant fees that have rendered legal aid work financially unviable for many firms and charities. Significant percentages of contracted offices have ceased taking new cases or stopped operating entirely, driven by an inability to recruit qualified lawyers and overwhelming administrative burdens. Consequently, there is a massive gap between the high demand for legal services and the limited supply of providers. This shortage creates profound regional inequalities and prevents vulnerable individuals from receiving necessary advice, undermining the principle of equal justice. This situation is highly relevant to open data because the systemic failure is only visible through the transparent release of procurement and case statistics. Without accessible, detailed datasets on provider activity and geographic availability, the scale of the collapse remains obscured. Open data initiatives expose the disparity between policy intentions and on-the-ground reality, highlighting how funding decisions directly impact service delivery. It underscores the necessity of monitoring public expenditure outcomes to ensure that legal rights are not eroded by silent systemic decay.
