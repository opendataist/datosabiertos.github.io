The article argues that government-driven digital data collection, often justified by efficiency, poses significant risks to individual liberty and security. While authorities may have benign intentions, the centralization of personal information creates attractive targets for data theft and misuse. This trend, exemplified by proposed digital learner IDs and past vaccine passport initiatives, reflects a broader ideological push toward a society where rights depend on state accreditation, echoing historical campaigns for compulsory identity cards. A core principle for handling public data is strict limitation to only what is necessary for specific services. The author contends that authorities frequently violate this norm by pooling databases or repurposing information, such as selling motorist data to private entities. This tendency to collate information beyond its original purpose undermines trust and exposes citizens to potential harm, demonstrating a systemic inability by the government to adequately protect sensitive records from leaks or unauthorized access. This perspective is highly relevant to open_data because it highlights the critical tension between data utility and privacy preservation. It underscores that open data initiatives must prioritize security and purpose limitation to avoid exacerbating the vulnerabilities inherent in centralized government systems. By advocating for minimal data retention and strict usage boundaries, the article provides a cautionary framework for ensuring that data transparency does not compromise individual rights or enable authoritarian overreach.
