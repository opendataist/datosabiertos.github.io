'Anonymized Data' Is A Gibberish Term, And Rampant Location Data Sales Is Still A Problem

The prevalent assumption that "anonymized" data ensures privacy is fundamentally flawed, as studies consistently demonstrate that location traces can be easily re-identified with minimal effort by cross-referencing other available datasets. This misconception allows companies and governments to dismiss serious privacy risks, creating a false sense of security while facilitating the widespread collection and sale of sensitive personal information without meaningful user consent or control. The commercial trade of location data operates within a highly opaque ecosystem dominated by unaccountable data brokers who profit from this lack of transparency. By obscuring the origins of the data and the extent of surveillance embedded in everyday applications, these entities evade public scrutiny and accountability. This deliberate opacity prevents users from understanding the true scale of their digital footprint, making it nearly impossible for individuals to exercise any real agency over how their movements and habits are tracked, monetized, and shared. This regulatory stagnation is particularly relevant to the open data movement, which often prioritizes data accessibility and reuse over individual privacy protections. Without robust federal privacy laws or effective oversight mechanisms, the open data community faces significant ethical challenges when utilizing datasets that are inherently linked to identifiable individuals. The continued abuse of this data highlights the critical need for open data initiatives to integrate strict privacy-preserving standards, ensuring that the pursuit of information utility does not come at the expense of fundamental civil liberties.
