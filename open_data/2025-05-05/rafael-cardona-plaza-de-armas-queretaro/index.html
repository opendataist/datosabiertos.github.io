Rafael Cardona - Plaza de Armas | Querétaro

Rafael Cardona - Plaza de Armas | Querétaro

The article critiques the strategic reclassification of the Pegasus spyware scandal in Mexico, where authorities and anti-corruption bodies deliberately excluded it from their jurisdiction by defining illegal surveillance as distinct from corruption. This legal maneuvering allowed the government to downplay a severe human rights violation, transforming a potential political crisis into a manageable administrative issue while avoiding accountability measures that might have triggered higher-level resignations or investigations. This reframing highlights a critical gap in open data and transparency frameworks: the lack of standardized, inclusive definitions for state abuses that bypass traditional economic corruption. When surveillance tools are excluded from anti-corruption catalogs, citizens lose a formal mechanism to demand data regarding government spending on such technologies and the specific targets of their deployment. Consequently, the fight for transparency is hindered because data on privacy violations remains obscured by semantic legal barriers rather than being subject to the rigorous scrutiny applied to financial misconduct. Furthermore, the piece illustrates how political actors opportunistically exploit these legal ambiguities for partisan gain, shifting focus toward electoral advantages rather than systemic justice. The relevance to open data lies in the urgent need for civil society to monitor not just financial trails, but the technological infrastructure of state control. Without clear, interoperable standards that categorize digital surveillance as a matter of public accountability, the gap between state secrecy and citizen oversight remains unbridgeable, allowing impunity to thrive through bureaucratic redefinition.
