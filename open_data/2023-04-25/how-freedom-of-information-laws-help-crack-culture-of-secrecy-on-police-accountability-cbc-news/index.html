How freedom of information laws help crack 'culture of secrecy' on police accountability | CBC News

This investigation reveals that police complaint and discipline records in Nova Scotia were previously inaccessible due to a culture of secrecy and restrictive data retention policies. By utilizing access to information laws, journalists uncovered internal decision-making processes, demonstrating that systematic transparency is crucial for understanding how law enforcement agencies investigate themselves and handle public grievances. The project highlights significant friction between public institutions and transparency advocates, particularly when agencies heavily redact documents under broad privacy claims. This struggle illustrates the challenges researchers and journalists face in verifying official narratives, as excessive withholding of non-sensitive operational details undermines the utility of freedom of information requests and delays meaningful oversight. Relevance to open data lies in proving that releasing structured complaint records enables external validation of police accountability. The findings show that most complaints are dismissed and repeated misconduct is common, arguments that rely on data visibility. Therefore, this case underscores the necessity of proactive data publishing and robust legal frameworks to ensure that public safety powers are balanced with independent, evidence-based scrutiny.
