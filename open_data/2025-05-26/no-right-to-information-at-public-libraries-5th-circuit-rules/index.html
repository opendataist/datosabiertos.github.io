No right to information at public libraries, 5th Circuit rules

No right to information at public libraries, 5th Circuit rules

The Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals ruled that public libraries do not violate patrons’ First Amendment rights by removing books based on their content. This decision overturns lower court judgments that had mandated the return of specific titles, establishing that libraries possess the editorial discretion to curate their collections as they see fit. The court emphasized that library officials are not obligated to maintain every book within their holdings and can exclude materials they deem inappropriate or outdated. A critical aspect of this ruling is the classification of library collection decisions as "government speech" rather than a venue for private expression. Consequently, the majority determined that patrons do not possess a constitutional right to receive information from government-funded sources. The judges argued that libraries function similarly to other curated institutions, such as museums or newspapers, where the entity selecting and presenting materials is exercising its own expressive rights, thereby insulating those curation choices from free speech challenges. This case is highly relevant to open data and digital rights communities because it sets a significant legal precedent regarding the control of public information access. It suggests that institutions holding public resources can restrict the availability of specific content without facing constitutional liability, potentially impacting how open archives and digital libraries manage controversial or disputed materials. The decision highlights the tension between preserving unrestricted access to information and allowing institutions to exercise subjective judgment over their collections.
