The Alberta government has introduced legislation to replace its existing privacy and freedom of information framework with a modernized approach that prioritizes strong data protection while adjusting access protocols. The new "Protection of Privacy Act" mandates a privacy-by-design philosophy, requiring public bodies to integrate privacy considerations into program design and adopt comprehensive management programs. It explicitly bans the sale of personal information and enforces stricter penalties for breaches, reflecting a significant shift toward heightened individual privacy safeguards in the digital age. Concurrently, the proposed "Access to Information Act" modifies how the public requests government records. It grants agencies broader discretion to reject requests deemed abusive, overly broad, or operationally disruptive, while also extending response timelines and expanding certain exemptions. These changes suggest a more defensive posture for public bodies when handling transparency requests, potentially limiting the ease and speed with which citizens can obtain government information compared to previous standards. This legislative overhaul is highly relevant to open data initiatives as it redefines the balance between transparency and privacy. By establishing rigorous new privacy controls and expanding exemptions for information access, the bill may restrict the availability of non-personal data for public use and research. Understanding these evolving regulatory boundaries is crucial for developers and policymakers aiming to create open data ecosystems that comply with Alberta’s stricter legal requirements while still fostering public innovation and accountability.
