The Punjab Police have sought an exemption to withhold quarterly statistics on crimes against women and children, arguing that public disclosure causes societal panic and hinders investigations. They cite a previous instance where an NGO allegedly leaked sensitive information, leading to widespread fear through media channels. Consequently, the police administration is attempting to classify this data as exempt from disclosure under specific legal provisions, despite the initial silence on the original Right to Information request. However, legal authorities and civil society organizations strongly oppose this move, asserting that transparency is essential for effective governance and victim support. Legal experts clarify that the specific section of the law cited by the police is irrelevant to this scenario, as it pertains to internal government advice rather than crime statistics. Experts emphasize that the crime itself, not the reporting of it, generates panic, and that hiding data prevents the public from understanding the true scale of gender-based violence and human trafficking issues. This conflict is critical to the open data movement because it highlights the tension between institutional secrecy and public accountability. Without accessible, verified data, non-profits cannot monitor law enforcement performance, provide targeted legal aid, or advocate for necessary policy reforms. Ensuring that crime statistics remain public enables stakeholders to track progress, hold authorities accountable, and draft evidence-based laws, thereby strengthening human rights protections rather than obstructing them.
