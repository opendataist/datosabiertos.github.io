El fotógrafo que lucha con Wikipedia por la imagen de un mono: “Me deben 52 millones de dólares”

The dispute between photographer David Slater and Wikimedia over the famous “monkey selfie” illustrates the complex definition of authorship in the digital ecosystem. Wikimedia maintains that, because there was no direct human intervention in the act of capturing the image, the photograph belongs to the public domain—a position Slater rejects, arguing that he technically controlled the equipment and framing. This disagreement exposes legal gaps in open-knowledge platforms when dealing with works whose conditions of creation do not fit neatly into traditional intellectual property categories. This precedent is crucial for the open-data and free-content movement because it establishes an interpretive criterion for determining which information can be freely shared without copyright restrictions. By ruling that the work is not owned by any human being, the platform prioritizes universal access to visual knowledge, but this decision has sparked controversy by overlooking the photographer’s creative and technical contributions. The dispute raises the question of whether “free access” policies should strictly uphold notions of individual authorship or prioritize the public interest over private rights—a delicate balance in the management of open repositories. Finally, the case gains renewed relevance today by anticipating the ethical and legal dilemmas posed by generative artificial intelligence. Just as the monkey is not considered a human author, works created solely by algorithms may also end up in the public domain, depriving their creators or developers of royalties. This underscores the need for open-source and free-content communities to update their regulatory frameworks to clearly distinguish between human intervention, automation, and algorithmic generation, ensuring that open-distribution models do not inadvertently infringe on intellectual property rights in the digital age.
