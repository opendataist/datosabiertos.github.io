This article highlights a significant gap in Michigan’s current transparency framework, as evidenced by the Attorney General’s recent review of the Ottawa County Board of Commissioners. Although the board’s sudden, unannounced changes to leadership and policies were deemed a blatant violation of the spirit of government transparency, they did not technically break existing state law. This outcome underscores the urgent need for legislative reforms to close loopholes, such as extending transparency obligations to officials who have been elected but not yet sworn in. The implications for open data and civic engagement are profound, as citizens currently rely on reactive measures rather than proactive access. The event emphasized that while the Attorney General’s office can investigate complaints, they cannot change statutes; therefore, strengthening these laws requires active pressure from the public on legislators. Furthermore, understanding what constitutes a public record—encompassing emails and various communications—is crucial for accountability, yet many residents remain unaware of the specific procedural steps, such as filing timely complaints with law enforcement or the state police. Relevance to open data lies in the tension between statutory limitations and the public’s right to know. When legal definitions fail to capture informal or newly elected officials, data remains inaccessible or incomplete, hindering democratic oversight. By advocating for broader definitions of public officials and records, the article supports a more robust open data ecosystem where transparency is enforced by design, not by accident. It serves as a call to action for communities to demand stricter enforcement mechanisms that align with the modern expectation of immediate, comprehensive governmental openness.
