Abolish FOIA

Abolish FOIA

The article argues that the U.S. Freedom of Information Act is fundamentally broken, plagued by excessive delays, bureaucratic abuse, and severe backlogs that render it ineffective for modern transparency. Rather than a functional tool for public accountability, the current system often requires litigation to compel agencies to release records, effectively privileging those with the resources to sue. This structural failure means that even basic requests for public data can take years, undermining the law’s original purpose and leaving citizens in the dark about government activities. To address these deficiencies, the author advocates for a paradigm shift from reactive disclosure to proactive publication, where agencies routinely release records unless specific redactions are required. This approach, potentially aided by automated review processes, would reduce the burden on both requesters and FOIA officers while fostering a culture of transparency. By making disclosure the default rather than the exception, the government could better handle the volume and complexity of digital records created today, moving beyond the limitations of an analog-era statute. Relevance to open data lies in the urgent need to modernize legal frameworks to match technological capabilities. The current FOIA model treats data access as a privilege granted after lengthy bureaucratic hurdles, whereas open data principles demand that public information be readily available by default. Reforming these laws to include strong enforcement mechanisms and proactive release protocols is essential for realizing the true potential of open data, ensuring that citizens can effectively access and utilize government information without prohibitive barriers.
