Open Data
ES EN

Colorado’s refusal to publish a comprehensive database of all certified law enforcement officers, including their employment histories, hinders public accountability and allows officers with prior misconduct to "wander" between agencies. Unlike most states that maintain transparent records, Colorado keeps this data secret, citing technical limitations and privacy concerns that experts argue are unfounded. This lack of transparency prevents journalists, defense attorneys, and the public from identifying officers with troubled pasts, thereby undermining effective oversight of police misconduct and hiring practices. The legal battle highlights a critical vulnerability in open data rights, where agencies may exploit restrictive criminal justice record laws to evade public scrutiny. Courts have upheld these denials by accepting claims that releasing data is too burdensome, despite evidence that the technology to do so exists. This sets a dangerous precedent where any government entity handling background checks could potentially classify itself under stricter privacy laws, effectively shielding employment and disciplinary data from public access under the guise of protecting undercover operations. This case is vital to the open data movement because it demonstrates how opaque records enable systemic failures in police accountability. Access to full certification and employment data allows stakeholders to assess officer credibility and track patterns of misconduct across jurisdictions. Without such transparency, communities cannot make informed decisions about who wields state power, risking the hiring of unfit officers during staffing shortages. Ensuring these datasets are publicly available is essential for maintaining trust in law enforcement and upholding the fundamental right to know about those serving the public.
