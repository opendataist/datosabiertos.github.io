Controversial FoI changes sparked after eSafety staff worried deluge of requests might ‘jam something’

Controversial FoI changes sparked after eSafety staff worried deluge of requests might ‘jam something’

Recent documents reveal that internal anxieties about email volume directly influenced the Australian government’s controversial proposal to restrict Freedom of Information access. Staff at the eSafety Commissioner were reportedly concerned that automated requests, arriving every few minutes, might overwhelm their systems. This internal panic provided the justification for legislative changes aimed at curbing what officials termed a "deluge" of frivolous or automated inquiries, highlighting how operational burdens can drive policy decisions that impact transparency. The proposed reforms included imposing fees for requests and banning anonymous submissions, arguments bolstered by claims that significant portions of these inquiries were generated by AI or coordinated tools. Government officials framed these measures as necessary to protect public servants from being overwhelmed by "online trolls" and potential foreign adversaries exploiting legitimate processes. However, critics argue that this narrative exaggerates the technical impact of automated requests to legitimize tighter controls, shifting the focus from efficient service delivery to limiting public scrutiny. This situation is critical to open data discussions as it illustrates the tension between bureaucratic efficiency and the fundamental right to access information. By attempting to restrict anonymous access and charging for requests, the government risks entrenching opacity and disproportionately hindering journalists, researchers, and citizens who rely on these mechanisms for accountability. The backlash suggests that solutions to administrative challenges should not come at the cost of democratic transparency, reinforcing the need for robust open data frameworks that prioritize public interest over institutional convenience.
