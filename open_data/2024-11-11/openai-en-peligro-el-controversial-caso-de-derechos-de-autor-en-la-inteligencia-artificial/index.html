¿Openai en peligro? el controversial caso de derechos de autor en la inteligencia artificial

A federal judge has temporarily dismissed a lawsuit filed by media outlets against OpenAI, setting an important precedent at the intersection of artificial intelligence and intellectual property. The ruling was based on the lack of evidence of concrete harm to the plaintiffs’ businesses, arguing that the removal of copyright metadata during the model’s training did not constitute an automatic infringement. This decision represents a strategic victory for the company, although it does not necessarily close the debate on the legality of the large-scale use of copyrighted content to develop generative technologies. The decision underscores the current difficulty in defining what constitutes actual harm in the context of AI-driven data synthesis. Because the court did not consider it likely that the system would faithfully reproduce the original content, it distinguished this case from allegations of direct copying. However, this leaves other lawsuits with different arguments open, such as those brought by The New York Times, indicating that the legal landscape remains unpredictable and dependent on how the specific use of the trained material is demonstrated. This case is relevant to open data because it marks a turning point in how digital information is protected and accessed. While AI requires large volumes of data to function, recent lawsuits are forcing a reevaluation of transparency and consent standards. The current ruling suggests that, without tangible evidence of commercial harm, access to and processing of public or licensed data could remain viable, thereby driving the need for clear legal frameworks that balance technological innovation with creators’ rights.
