Open Data: Democracy’s Tool or Big Tech’s Fuel?

Open data gives people quick access to raw information. This helps them make smart choices without waiting for others. Researchers at the University of Oxford show how this works. They use standard farm data from FAOSTAT. This lets experts compare food security in nearly 100 countries.

This transparency helps citizens check government claims. It holds institutions accountable. For example, UK citizens used special requests to find unclaimed pension funds. This helped widows avoid money losses from government mistakes. Also, the Northern Ireland Department of Health shares detailed worker stats. The public can use this to check staffing levels. They can also spot problems in the healthcare system. These examples show that open data strengthens democracy. It ensures public resources are used correctly.

However, open data also brings privacy and security risks. Big tech companies like Meta and Google scrape public web content. They use this data to train AI models. They often ignore rules like robots.txt. This practice reduces user consent. It shifts control from people to big companies. A study by the Electronic Frontier Foundation shows how this works. It compromises informed consent. Users may not know how their digital footprints help commercial algorithms.

The future of open data platforms is also uncertain. The COVID-19 Data Portal closed after the pandemic. This shows how projects can become outdated when public interest drops. This leaves users with broken access to important history. Users must also think about the ethics of reusing data. New blockchain platforms like Story try to pay creators. While these tools protect intellectual property, they make sharing information harder. Users expect open and easy access. These tools complicate that flow. As a result, users face a choice. They must balance the convenience of easy information with the loss of control over their personal and creative data.
