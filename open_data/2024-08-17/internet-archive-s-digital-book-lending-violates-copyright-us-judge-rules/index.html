Internet Archive's digital book lending violates copyright, US judge rules

A U.S. judge ruled that the Internet Archive infringed copyright by scanning and lending digital copies of protected books, rejecting its claim of fair use. The court determined that these digital acts replaced authorized licensing for traditional libraries, establishing that while physical books can be lent, bulk digitization without permission violates copyright laws. This decision significantly impacts the concept of controlled digital lending, a model where nonprofits digitize materials to expand access. By denying fair use protection for this practice, the ruling limits how digital archives can operate when dealing with copyrighted works, potentially restricting the availability of scholarly and popular texts that are not in the public domain. The case is crucial for open data and digital preservation because it sets a legal precedent for managing digital cultural heritage. It highlights the tension between maximizing public access to information and respecting intellectual property rights. For open data advocates, this underscores the need for clear legal frameworks to protect digital archives while ensuring they comply with existing copyright regulations to maintain sustainability and legitimacy.
