The article illustrates the dual nature of the Freedom of Information Act as both a vital investigative tool and a significant bureaucratic obstacle. While journalists frequently rely on FOIA requests to uncover critical data for public interest stories, the process is often plagued by extreme delays and excessive redactions. These administrative burdens can render obtained information nearly useless, forcing reporters into lengthy legal battles that drain the financial and temporal resources of most news organizations. The author details a specific case involving the FDA and Amazon, seeking evidence of high-level communication regarding NAC dietary supplements. The delayed response confirmed that the Office of the Commissioner had not tipped off Amazon about regulatory actions, debunking conspiracy theories about prior coordination. However, the two-year wait for this clarity highlights the inefficiency of current systems, where timely journalism is often sacrificed to procedural inertia, potentially allowing important regulatory narratives to solidify without immediate public scrutiny. This narrative is highly relevant to open data advocates because it underscores the gap between legal access to information and its practical utility. True open data requires not just the existence of laws, but timely, accessible, and unredacted datasets that enable real-time accountability. When government responses are delayed by years, the public interest is compromised, demonstrating the urgent need for reforms that prioritize speed and transparency in digital record-keeping and release protocols.
