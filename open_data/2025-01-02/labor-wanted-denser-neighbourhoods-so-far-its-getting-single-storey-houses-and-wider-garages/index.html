Labor wanted denser neighbourhoods. So far, it’s getting single-storey houses and wider garages

NSW’s Transport-Oriented Development policy, designed to increase housing density near train stations, has seen minimal success, with developers favoring low-density projects like garages and single-family homes over high-rise apartments. This outcome highlights a critical disconnect between government zoning mandates and market realities, as soaring construction costs and land prices render many proposed zones economically unviable for developers. The policy’s failure to stimulate significant supply suggests that legislative changes alone cannot overcome fundamental economic barriers to urban infill. The broader implications for open data are profound, as this case underscores the necessity of transparent, accessible planning data to validate policy effectiveness. When government initiatives rely on vague spatial designations without clear, granular data on developer behavior and feasibility, it becomes difficult to hold policymakers accountable or identify why specific areas are being underutilized. Open data frameworks must move beyond simple mapping to include detailed transaction and application metrics, allowing for a deeper understanding of the gap between intended outcomes and actual development patterns. Ultimately, this situation demonstrates that housing policy requires a nuanced approach grounded in economic feasibility and robust infrastructure planning, rather than arbitrary geographic circles. The reliance on such a limited strategy risks exacerbating supply shortages if not complemented by comprehensive reforms addressing affordability and local council capabilities. For the open data community, this serves as a powerful example of how data transparency can reveal the limitations of top-down planning, urging stakeholders to demand more sophisticated, evidence-based approaches to solving complex urban challenges.
