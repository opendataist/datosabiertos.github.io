'Anonymized Data' Is A Gibberish Term, And Rampant Location Data Sales Is Still A Problem

The article argues that the widespread reliance on "anonymized" data is a dangerous illusion, as studies consistently prove that location information can easily be re-identified with minimal effort. Despite claims from companies and officials that stripping personal identifiers renders data harmless, the intricate nature of movement patterns allows individuals to be traced back to their real identities. This false sense of security leaves users vulnerable to surveillance by stalkers, law enforcement, and data brokers who operate with little transparency or user consent. This issue is particularly relevant to open data initiatives because it highlights a critical contradiction: while open data advocates often promote the reuse of public datasets for societal benefit, many corporate data ecosystems currently thrive on opacity and deception. The lack of transparency in how data brokers like Veraset acquire and sell location records undermines the trust necessary for ethical data sharing. When data sources are hidden or misrepresented, it becomes impossible to verify provenance, assess bias, or ensure that open datasets respect the privacy and rights of the individuals they represent. Ultimately, regulatory efforts have stalled due to intense industry lobbying, allowing these practices to continue unchecked until a major scandal forces change. The persistence of these systems demonstrates that without robust legal frameworks and genuine transparency, open data principles cannot be effectively applied to location tracking. To truly serve the public interest, open data movements must confront the reality that current data collection models often exploit users under the guise of anonymity, rather than empowering them through clear, informed, and accountable data practices.
