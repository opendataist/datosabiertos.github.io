'Anonymized Data' Is A Gibberish Term, And Rampant Location Data Sales Is Still A Problem

The widespread belief that removing direct identifiers creates "anonymous" data is a dangerous misconception. Research consistently demonstrates that location data can be easily re-identified by cross-referencing it with other available datasets, rendering the promise of privacy protection largely ineffective. This false sense of security allows companies to continue collecting and selling highly sensitive information under the guise of harmless, aggregated metrics, despite the clear potential for misuse by stalkers, law enforcement, and other third parties. A major barrier to accountability is the opaque nature of the data brokerage industry. Many intermediaries operate with extreme secrecy, refusing to disclose the sources of the data they trade or how it was originally collected. This lack of transparency prevents users from understanding or controlling how their digital footprints are monetized. The argument that anonymity shields the public is further undermined by the reality that location traces reveal intimate details about daily life, making it trivial to link such data back to specific individuals, thereby exposing them to significant privacy risks. This article is critical to open_data discussions because it highlights the urgent need for transparency and accountability in data ecosystems. The stagnation of meaningful privacy legislation, driven by powerful lobbying efforts, suggests that without rigorous standards for data provenance and usage, the current model of unchecked data harvesting will persist. It underscores that open data initiatives must prioritize genuine privacy safeguards and clear disclosure mechanisms to prevent the exploitation of personal information, rather than relying on ineffective de-identification practices that fail to protect citizens.
