The article highlights the persistent challenges in exercising the right to information in Pakistan, illustrating how bureaucratic resistance and harassment often deter citizens and journalists from seeking transparency. Although regulatory bodies like the Information Commission can intervene to correct misconduct, the underlying culture of secrecy remains entrenched, with officials frequently employing obstructions or legal threats to avoid disclosure. This environment creates a significant barrier to accountability, forcing advocates to navigate a system designed to resist openness. A critical issue identified is the lack of standardized, reliable procedures for handling information requests, alongside insufficient capacity among both public servants and commission staff. Officials often lack the training to understand their legal obligations, while commissions struggle with resource constraints and inconsistent enforcement. The result is a disjointed process where valid requests are delayed, ignored, or met with irrelevant data, undermining the effectiveness of oversight mechanisms and leaving citizens without guaranteed access to public records. For open data advocates, this narrative underscores that technical availability of data is insufficient without a supportive legal and cultural framework. True transparency requires proactive disclosure policies and strong political commitment to shift the bureaucratic mindset from secrecy to custodianship. Improving open data ecosystems demands not only better record-keeping and training but also the empowerment of oversight bodies to enforce accountability, ensuring that information remains a public asset rather than a guarded privilege.
