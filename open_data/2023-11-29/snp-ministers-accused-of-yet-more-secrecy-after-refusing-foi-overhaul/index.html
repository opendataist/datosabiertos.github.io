SNP ministers accused of yet more secrecy after refusing FoI overhaul

The Scottish Government’s refusal to amend Freedom of Information laws to include confidentiality clauses in public contracts and extend coverage to private and third-sector service providers has sparked significant political controversy. By rejecting immediate legislative expansion, officials argue that existing frameworks are sufficiently rigorous for ensuring accountability, preferring to rely on current provisions and future consultations rather than enacting new primary legislation. This stance reflects a broader institutional resistance to expanding the scope of transparency measures beyond traditional public authorities. Opposition parties and campaigners condemn this approach as a deliberate delay tactic, asserting that publicly funded organizations currently evade scrutiny due to loopholes in ownership definitions. Critics emphasize that the lack of commitment to include these entities undermines democratic principles and public trust, labeling the government’s response as timid and secretive. The debate highlights a critical tension between maintaining administrative status quo and adapting legal structures to ensure comprehensive oversight of modern service delivery models. This article is highly relevant to open_data because it illustrates the persistent challenges in achieving true transparency when essential services are delivered by non-governmental entities. It underscores how legal definitions of "public authority" directly impact the accessibility of data, creating significant gaps in information availability. Understanding these legislative barriers is crucial for advocates seeking to expand open data principles, as it reveals that technical data availability is often secondary to the political will to enforce broader institutional accountability.
