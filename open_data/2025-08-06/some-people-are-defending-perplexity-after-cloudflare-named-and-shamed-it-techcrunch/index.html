Some people are defending Perplexity after Cloudflare ‘named and shamed’ it | TechCrunch

Some people are defending Perplexity after Cloudflare ‘named and shamed’ it | TechCrunch

The conflict between Cloudflare and Perplexity highlights a critical tension in the evolving digital ecosystem: whether AI agents acting on user behalf should enjoy the same access rights as human browsers. Cloudflare argues that evading explicit blocking mechanisms constitutes malicious behavior, akin to hacking, while Perplexity and its defenders contend that accessing public information upon user request is a legitimate function of digital assistance. This disagreement underscores a fundamental question about the nature of agency on the open web and whether automated tools should be held to the same transparency and consent standards as human interaction. This debate is particularly relevant to open data because it challenges the traditional assumption that websites can unilaterally control who accesses their public content. As AI traffic increasingly dominates internet activity, the use of standards like Web Bot Auth becomes essential for distinguishing between legitimate agents and scrapers. If AI agents are denied access to public data without cryptographic consent, the very definition of an "open" internet shifts, potentially creating walled gardens where content visibility depends on proprietary agreements rather than public availability. The implications extend to the sustainability of the open web itself. If website owners aggressively block AI agents to protect traffic and ad revenue, the flow of information to training models and user queries could be severely restricted. Conversely, if agents bypass restrictions, data sovereignty and site integrity are compromised. The outcome of this struggle will likely determine whether the future internet remains a shared, accessible resource or fragments into restricted silos governed by complex bot-management protocols.
