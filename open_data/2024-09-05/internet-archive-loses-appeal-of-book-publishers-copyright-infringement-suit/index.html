Internet Archive Loses Appeal of Book Publishers’ Copyright Infringement Suit

Internet Archive Loses Appeal of Book Publishers’ Copyright Infringement Suit

A U.S. appeals court has rejected the Internet Archive’s legal challenge, confirming that its controlled digital lending program violates copyright law. The ruling establishes that scanning owned print books to create digital versions for lending does not qualify as "fair use," as the digital copies serve the same purpose as the originals and lack transformative value. This decision affirms that creators and publishers retain the right to control and profit from their work, rejecting the argument that digital lending functions identically to traditional physical borrowing. The significance of this ruling extends beyond legal technicalities, highlighting the tension between preserving cultural heritage and respecting intellectual property rights. The court explicitly rejected the Internet Archive’s claim to traditional library exemptions, noting that mass digitization and distribution of derivative works differ fundamentally from the limited copy-making allowed for preservation. This distinction implies that organizations seeking to provide universal access to knowledge must operate within stricter boundaries, ensuring that widespread digital distribution does not undermine the economic incentives for creating new creative works. This case is crucial for open data and digital preservation communities because it sets a precedent for how cultural institutions can legally share digitized materials. It warns that initiatives relying on mass scanning and unrestricted digital access may face significant legal risks and liability. Consequently, open data advocates and libraries must prioritize licensing agreements and fair use compliance to ensure their digital collections remain sustainable and legally viable, balancing the ideal of open access with the necessity of compensating content creators.
