Major food corporations have established a new transparency baseline by publicly disclosing their food waste figures, marking a significant shift toward accountability in an industry historically resistant to such scrutiny. By reporting hundreds of thousands of tonnes of waste, these companies aim to create a standardized metric to track progress against 2030 reduction goals. This move highlights the critical role of open data in driving corporate sustainability, as transparent reporting serves as a catalyst for identifying inefficiencies and accelerating collective action to mitigate environmental impacts. However, the reliability of this initial dataset is limited by the exclusion of several key players and the anonymized nature of the submissions. Critics argue that while voluntary disclosure is a step forward, it falls short of the mandatory, granular data required for effective public oversight and policy-making. The tension between industry self-regulation and government mandates illustrates a broader struggle within open data initiatives: balancing corporate convenience with the public’s right to comprehensive, verifiable information that accurately reflects the scale of global resource mismanagement. This article is relevant to open data as it underscores the ongoing challenge of transforming voluntary corporate disclosures into standardized, accessible, and usable public datasets. It demonstrates how the lack of granular, mandatory reporting hinders the ability of researchers, policymakers, and consumers to fully understand and address systemic issues. Ultimately, the situation reinforces the necessity for open, raw data streams that allow for independent analysis, ensuring that sustainability claims can be objectively verified rather than merely announced.
