The article highlights significant flaws in Saskatchewan’s Freedom of Information system, where prohibitive costs, excessive delays, and weak enforcement mechanisms severely hinder public access to government records. High fees deter ordinary citizens from seeking accountability, while public bodies frequently exceed deadlines or over-redact information. The system’s ineffectiveness is compounded by the fact that the Information and Privacy Commissioner can only issue non-binding recommendations, leaving citizens with little recourse other than expensive legal action. This lack of robust oversight directly undermines the core principles of open data by creating a culture of secrecy rather than transparency. When governments withhold information or impose financial barriers, they prevent the public from scrutinizing decision-making processes. The recurring non-compliance by public bodies suggests a systemic effort to retain control over information, which erodes trust in institutional accountability and limits the societal benefits of accessible, timely data. The piece is relevant to open data because it illustrates how legislative frameworks must evolve beyond mere existence to ensure practical, equitable access. It underscores the necessity for modernized laws that include stronger enforcement powers and digital accessibility standards. Without such reforms, the promise of transparent governance remains theoretical, as those without substantial resources are effectively excluded from exercising their right to know, thereby perpetuating information asymmetry and hindering true openness.
