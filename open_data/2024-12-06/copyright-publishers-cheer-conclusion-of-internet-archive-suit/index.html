Copyright: Publishers Cheer Conclusion of Internet Archive Suit

The recent resolution of Hachette v. Internet Archive marks a definitive legal victory for traditional publishers, rejecting the concept of “controlled digital lending” as copyright infringement. By declining to petition the Supreme Court, the Internet Archive accepted the Second Circuit’s ruling that creating unlicensed digital copies of physical books violates existing laws. This decision establishes that mere format conversion is not transformative, thereby protecting the market value of authorized ebooks and ensuring that authors and publishers retain control over how their works are distributed in digital environments. This outcome reinforces the principle that copyright holders must set the terms for digital access, prioritizing economic incentives for creators over unrestricted public availability. The court emphasized that allowing such widespread, unlicensed lending would discourage artistic production and harm the public interest in long-term cultural progress. Consequently, platforms cannot bypass licensing agreements by claiming to substitute physical ownership with digital loans, a practice that was previously challenged as a means to expand access to global literary collections. This case is critical for open data discourse as it delineates the boundary between public access and proprietary rights within the digital economy. It warns against treating copyrighted creative works as open resources for unrestricted harvesting or AI training without consent. For researchers and developers, the ruling underscores the necessity of respecting intellectual property frameworks when accessing digital content, highlighting that “open” initiatives must operate within legal constraints to remain sustainable and ethically sound.
