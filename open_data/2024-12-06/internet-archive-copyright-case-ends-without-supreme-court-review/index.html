Internet Archive Copyright Case Ends Without Supreme Court Review

The Internet Archive has concluded its lengthy legal battle with major publishers by declining to appeal the Second Circuit’s ruling that its controlled digital lending program constitutes copyright infringement. This decision effectively settles the case through a consent judgment, resulting in a financial payment to cover the publishers' legal costs. By choosing not to pursue Supreme Court review, the Archive accepts the precedent that scanning and lending copyrighted books, even under strict one-to-one ratios, does not qualify as fair use. The core implication of this ruling is that authors and publishers retain the exclusive right to determine how their works are distributed digitally, regardless of the lender’s nonprofit status. The court’s definitive stance reinforces the traditional copyright model, prioritizing the economic rights of creators over the Internet Archive’s mission to expand public access. This outcome significantly limits the legal viability of controlled digital lending initiatives, setting a restrictive boundary for how libraries and archives can legally share digitized materials with the public. This case is highly relevant to open data and open access communities because it challenges the fundamental premise that non-commercial entities can freely digitize and distribute cultural heritage. It highlights the tension between open dissemination and proprietary copyright controls, serving as a cautionary tale for digital preservation efforts. The ruling suggests that without explicit permission, initiatives aiming to make copyrighted knowledge openly available face substantial legal risks, potentially hindering the free flow of information and the long-term preservation of digital works.
