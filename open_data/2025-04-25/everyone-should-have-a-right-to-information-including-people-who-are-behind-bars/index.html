Everyone should have a right to information, including people who are behind bars

Everyone should have a right to information, including people who are behind bars

The article highlights a critical systemic failure in the Canadian correctional system, where the denial of internet access and reliable information sources to incarcerated individuals severely hinders rehabilitation and reintegration. This information blackout contradicts the stated policy goals of the Corrections and Conditional Release Act, which emphasizes reforming offenders. By isolating prisoners from essential digital resources, the system inadvertently reinforces cycles of disadvantage and increases the likelihood of reoffending, thereby undermining public safety and social equity. A significant implication of this restriction is the effective barrier it places against post-secondary education, which is now predominantly delivered online. Without internet access, inmates cannot pursue degrees or develop the digital literacy required in the modern workforce. This lack of technical proficiency leaves individuals ill-equipped to handle basic societal functions, such as job applications or medical appointments, upon release. Consequently, the current approach fails to provide the proven tools necessary for self-sufficiency, ignoring evidence that education drastically reduces recidivism rates. This issue is relevant to open_data advocates because it underscores the fundamental principle that access to information is a basic human right, not a privilege. The article demonstrates how restricting data flow creates tangible social harms, particularly for marginalized populations. It calls for a shift toward transparency and equity, suggesting that opening information channels—including digital access—can break cycles of poverty and crime. Ultimately, treating information as a public good within correctional settings aligns with broader open_data goals of fostering an informed, capable, and equitable society.
