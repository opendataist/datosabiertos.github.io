Hawaii Health Department tells child-grooming “therapists” to hide LGBTQ identity from parents – NaturalNews.com

The article details a controversial policy at the University of Hawaii’s Center for Cognitive Behavior Therapy, which advises clinicians against documenting LGBTQ+ statuses in patient records due to potential parental access. This guidance stems from Hawaii’s laws allowing minors aged 14 and older to consent to mental health services independently, creating a scenario where private medical notes could inadvertently reveal a youth’s identity to guardians. The training materials emphasize that while being LGBTQ+ is not a risk, disclosure can be, urging staff to exercise extreme caution in their documentation practices to protect the privacy and safety of their clients. This situation is highly relevant to open data because it illustrates the critical tension between privacy protections and the accessibility of public or institutional records. It highlights how data governance, even within private healthcare settings, intersects with transparency laws like FOIA. The release of these internal training documents demonstrates that administrative decisions regarding what information is recorded and how it is protected are subject to public scrutiny. Furthermore, it raises questions about how institutions balance individual privacy rights against the need for transparent oversight of their operational guidelines and ethical standards. The core implication is that data documentation policies are not merely administrative but have significant personal consequences. The article suggests that the drive to create "affirming" environments has led to restrictive guidelines on record-keeping, potentially impacting the completeness and accuracy of health data. This underscores the importance of clear, consistent data standards that respect both individual confidentiality and the integrity of the information ecosystem. It serves as a case study in how social and political contexts can directly influence data collection practices, raising concerns about the potential loss of valuable demographic health information for research and public health monitoring.
