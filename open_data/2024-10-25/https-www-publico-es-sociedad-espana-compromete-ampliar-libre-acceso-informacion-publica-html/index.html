The Spanish Government’s ratification of the Council of Europe Convention on Access to Official Documents (Tromsø Convention) represents a significant, albeit delayed, step toward modernizing public transparency. This international treaty obliges Spain to reform its current Transparency Law to broaden access to information, including categories that have previously been opaque, such as internal communications and archived documentation. However, ratification and the ensuing legal transformation will not be immediate, prolonging uncertainty about the actual timeline for implementing these structural improvements. The significance of this agreement lies in its ability to address historical deficiencies in the Spanish system, where opacity and subjective interpretation of regulations have led to lengthy judicial processes just to obtain basic information. The Convention introduces key principles, such as the requirement to demonstrate actual harm in order to deny access and the prioritization of the public interest, thereby establishing more rigorous standards than those currently in force. These provisions aim to curb the arbitrary use of secret classifications and ensure that citizens have accessible avenues for complaint before resorting to the courts. For the open data community, this accession is crucial because it establishes a binding framework that recognizes public information as a fundamental right, rather than merely an administrative concession. By aligning with international standards, Spain commits to enhancing accountability and citizen participation. Although legislative changes are slow, the political commitment secured lays the necessary groundwork for a more robust, transparent, and rights-respecting open data culture, one that better safeguards citizens’ rights vis-à-vis state power.
