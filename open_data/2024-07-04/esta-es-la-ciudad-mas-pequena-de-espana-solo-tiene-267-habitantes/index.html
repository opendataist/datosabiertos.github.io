Esta es la ciudad más pequeña de España: solo tiene 267 habitantes

Frías, located in the province of Burgos, is recognized as Spain’s smallest city, a status stemming from a 15th-century royal grant rather than contemporary demographic criteria. With fewer than three hundred inhabitants—well below the modern threshold of ten thousand typically required for urban classification—its historical title remains valid. This highlights a significant divergence between administrative definitions based on population density and economic activity, and historical designations preserved for cultural heritage reasons. The case of Frías illustrates how open data systems often struggle to reconcile historical anomalies with standardized statistical categories. Official geographic and statistical institutes generally define cities based on continuous urbanization and service-oriented economies. However, Frías serves as a critical exception, demonstrating that official datasets must account for legacy classifications. This discrepancy raises important questions about how digital public records categorize administrative entities, suggesting that rigid numerical criteria may overlook valuable historical context and legal precedents established centuries ago. This example is highly relevant to open data initiatives because it underscores the necessity of integrating qualitative historical metadata with quantitative demographic statistics. To accurately reflect territorial reality, open data platforms cannot rely solely on current population counts; they must also preserve and display historical legal statuses. Understanding such exceptions is crucial for policymakers, researchers, and citizens who depend on accurate, nuanced geographic information to make informed decisions about cultural preservation and administrative governance.
