Hallan a los responsables genéticos de la resistencia a los antibióticos en bacterias

Hallan a los responsables genéticos de la resistencia a los antibióticos en bacterias

A century-long study of bacterial evolution reveals that a small minority of plasmids are primarily responsible for the global spread of multidrug resistance. By analyzing thousands of historical and current samples across six continents, researchers discovered that these transferable genetic structures evolved to acquire antibiotic resistance genes over the last hundred years. Consequently, a limited group of modern plasmids now acts as a major threat by conferring resistance to both first-line and last-resort treatments, driving a health crisis that claims at least a million lives annually. The research highlights how ancestral plasmids, initially devoid of resistance traits, adapted to the antibiotic era through genetic insertion or fusion events. This evolutionary shift transformed them into potent vectors for antimicrobial resistance genes. Understanding this historical trajectory allows scientists to model how resistance might evolve in the future, providing crucial insights for predicting disease outbreaks and informing public health strategies to mitigate the growing threat of untreatable infections. This article is highly relevant to open data because it relies on the largest existing dataset of its kind, emphasizing the critical role of accessible, large-scale biological data in tackling global health challenges. The study demonstrates how aggregating and analyzing diverse, longitudinal datasets can uncover patterns invisible to smaller-scale research. Ultimately, it underscores that open, comprehensive data sharing is essential for developing predictive models and effective interventions against antibiotic resistance, benefiting both scientific communities and public health policymakers worldwide.
