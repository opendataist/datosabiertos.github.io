Arbitrarily Labelling Individuals As Habitual Offenders Not Only Taints Them But Also Impairs Their Fundamental Rights: J&K High Court

Arbitrarily Labelling Individuals As Habitual Offenders Not Only Taints Them But Also Impairs Their Fundamental Rights: J&K High Court

The court ruled that police actions to label individuals as "history-sheeters" and maintain surveillance records must strictly adhere to procedural fairness and legal parameters, rather than being arbitrary. By highlighting the severe social stigma and privacy violations these actions cause to individuals and their families, the judgment emphasizes that such intrusive measures require justification and must respect constitutional rights. This decision serves as a critical check on police discretion, ensuring that preventative measures do not undermine personal dignity. A core implication of this ruling is the mandatory requirement for due process, specifically the obligation to hear objections before entering a person’s name in a surveillance register. The court criticized the failure to issue show-cause notices or allow the petitioner to contest his classification, establishing that administrative efficiency cannot supersede the right to be heard. This establishes a precedent that police discretion must be exercised according to reason and justice, preventing unilateral decisions that lack transparency or evidence. This case is highly relevant to open data because it underscores the risks associated with unverified or improperly governed administrative databases. When sensitive information regarding criminal history is stored without rigorous procedural safeguards, it can lead to irreversible harm to citizens' reputations and privacy. The judgment advocates for transparency and accountability in data management, suggesting that any public or semi-public database containing such sensitive personal data must include mechanisms for verification, notice, and recourse to protect individuals from arbitrary state action.
