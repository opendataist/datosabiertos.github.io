Explained: Scotland's literacy and numeracy statistics

The article argues that replacing objective, sample-based assessments with teacher-submitted data has severely compromised the reliability of Scottish education statistics. By eliminating standardized tests in favor of subjective judgments, the system lacks clear, consistent standards, making national comparisons problematic. This shift has created significant ambiguity regarding what proficiency levels actually represent, undermining the validity of the data used for public reporting and policy decisions. The reliance on teacher submissions introduces substantial risks of bias and manipulation driven by political pressure. With performance data linked directly to schools, educators face immense incentives to inflate results to demonstrate improvement, even without genuine evidence of student progress. This environment fosters subconscious data gaming, where the pursuit of favorable statistics often outweighs the accurate reflection of student attainment, ultimately distorting the perceived state of the education system. This is critical for open data because it highlights how the removal of transparent, independent measurement mechanisms erodes public trust and analytical utility. Without robust, sample-based data like the former Scottish Survey of Literacy and Numeracy, stakeholders cannot accurately evaluate curriculum effectiveness or attainment trends. The current system obscures reality, preventing informed decision-making and suggesting that political motivations may have prioritized controllable metrics over genuine educational insight.
