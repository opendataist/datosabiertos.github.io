DC's Daunting Task to Boot, Impound Cars With Unpaid Tickets

Washington D.C. faces a significant enforcement gap, as only a small fraction of vehicles with substantial unpaid photo-enforcement tickets are actually booted or impounded. Although tens of thousands of cars are eligible for action due to accumulated debts, logistical constraints like limited impound space and staffing, along with policy decisions to avoid confrontational street enforcement, severely hinder full compliance. This disparity highlights the challenges in translating legal authority into effective operational results, leaving many violators beyond the reach of immediate physical deterrents. The inability to revoke driver’s licenses and restrict tag renewals to D.C.-registered vehicles further complicates accountability, particularly since most offending cars hail from neighboring Maryland and Virginia. These limitations prevent the District from leveraging its strongest administrative levers against out-of-state owners, rendering standard collection methods often ineffective. Consequently, the system relies heavily on sending debts to collections, yet statutory restrictions prohibit reporting delinquencies to credit bureaus or garnishing wages, which weakens the financial pressure intended to compel payment. This case is critical to open data discussions because it reveals how transparency requests expose the chasm between legislative intent and bureaucratic reality. By making operational metrics public, such data underscores the need for systemic reform, such as expanding enforcement to private property and updating state laws. It demonstrates that while open data can illuminate inefficiencies and accountability failures, meaningful change requires overcoming structural barriers that prevent the government from fully executing its regulatory duties despite having the necessary information.
