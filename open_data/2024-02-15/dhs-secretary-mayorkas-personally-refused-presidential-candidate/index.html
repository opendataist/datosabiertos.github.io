DHS Secretary Mayorkas personally refused presidential candidate...

The core revelation of this report is that Homeland Security Secretary Alejandro Mayorkas exercised his broad legal discretion to deny Secret Service protection to presidential candidate Robert F. Kennedy Jr., despite the candidate’s campaign asserting he met statutory criteria for major candidate status. This decision was made in consultation with key congressional leadership, highlighting that protection eligibility is not an automatic right but a determined designation subject to high-level administrative judgment and political consultation. The underlying implication involves the tension between government discretion and the perceived necessity of safeguarding political figures amid increasing threats. By forcing the disclosure of these records through litigation, the case underscores the opacity surrounding executive branch decisions on security matters. It suggests that without public pressure or legal compulsion, the specific rationales for denying protection—even when threats are cited—remain hidden from view, raising concerns about accountability in candidate safety protocols. This story is relevant to open data because it exemplifies how Freedom of Information Act litigation serves as a critical mechanism for uncovering non-public government records. It demonstrates that vital information regarding executive decisions, internal communications, and threat assessments is often inaccessible without proactive legal action. Consequently, it reinforces the importance of transparent data practices, showing that the public’s right to understand the criteria and processes behind national security decisions for political candidates often depends on the persistence of researchers and journalists in demanding these records.
