Surveillance Register Entries Can't Be Based On Police Likes Or Dislikes: Rajasthan High Court Condemns Arbitrary Opening Of History Sheets

Surveillance Register Entries Can't Be Based On Police Likes Or Dislikes: Rajasthan High Court Condemns Arbitrary Opening Of History Sheets

The Rajasthan High Court ruled that police authorities cannot arbitrarily add individuals to surveillance registers or open history sheets based merely on the registration of criminal cases. The judgment emphasizes that such actions require reasonable belief grounded in strong evidence, rather than subjective discretion or private opinion. By setting aside an order against a petitioner who was not a habitual offender, the court established that the criteria for inclusion must be objective and legally justified, protecting citizens from unfounded state surveillance. This decision clarifies that the power to classify someone as a habitual offender is strictly limited by statutory definitions and rules of reason. The court highlighted that merely being accused is insufficient; there must be a legitimate basis, such as prior convictions or credible intelligence regarding criminal habits. This ensures that administrative powers are exercised within legal bounds, preventing the misuse of surveillance mechanisms to harass individuals who have been acquitted or had charges dropped. The ruling is significant for open data and public accountability because it establishes legal precedents regarding who can be recorded in state surveillance databases. It underscores the importance of transparency and due process in the creation and maintenance of these records, ensuring they reflect verified legal status rather than police suspicion. By linking arbitrary inclusion to violations of fundamental rights, the judgment reinforces the need for accurate, lawful, and auditable data systems in law enforcement.
