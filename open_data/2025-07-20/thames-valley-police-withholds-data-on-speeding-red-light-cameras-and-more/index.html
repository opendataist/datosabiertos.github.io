Thames Valley Police withholds data on speeding, red light cameras and more

Thames Valley Police withholds data on speeding, red light cameras and more

Thames Valley Police’s refusal to release specific details via Freedom of Information requests highlights significant gaps in institutional transparency compared to peer forces. Unlike Dorset Police, which provided comprehensive data, Thames Valley cited technical and financial constraints, claiming information was not easily retrievable or too costly to process. This selective openness undermines the public’s right to scrutinize police operations, from speeding enforcement to administrative expenditures, suggesting that structural inefficiencies rather than genuine security concerns may be masking accountability failures. The disparity in outcomes demonstrates that open data is less about the inherent complexity of the information and more about an organization’s willingness to organize and share it. When authorities claim data cannot be extracted efficiently, it often reflects poor data management practices rather than legitimate operational barriers. This distinction is critical for understanding how public bodies handle their digital assets and whether they are meeting their duty to maintain accessible records for community oversight. This article is relevant to open data because it illustrates the tangible consequences of closed or poorly managed information systems. It shows that without robust data architecture, even legally mandated transparency mechanisms can fail, allowing public institutions to obscure their activities. True open data requires proactive organization and accessibility, ensuring that citizens can effectively hold power to account, rather than relying on authorities to interpret which information is "easy" to provide.
