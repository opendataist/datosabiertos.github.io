The Double-Edged Sword of Open Data: Accountability, Risks, and Access

Open data changes how we learn. Instead of just watching, we can study facts ourselves. This helps us understand how the world works. For example, the Australian Bureau of Statistics shares detailed economic data. This lets people check if national growth helps real families. It shows the gap between big numbers and personal money.

The Food and Agriculture Organization also shares data. This helps us track progress on global goals. We can hold countries accountable for fighting poverty and hunger. In Montana, data on drug seizures helps communities ask for more funding. In the UK, data on illegal vapes helps keep people safe. In Wisconsin, open records show how well police are held responsible.

However, open data has risks. The same access that helps us can also hurt us. It can threaten our privacy. For instance, a company called Worldcoin collected biometric data. A privacy agency ruled against this. It showed how sensitive data can harm personal dignity.

Bad actors can also misuse technology. Deepfake pornography is a serious problem. It uses AI tools to create fake images. This shows that open technology without rules can cause harm.

Some companies limit access to their best tools. OpenAI, for example, restricts its advanced AI models. This creates a divide. Some people have access, while others do not. This limits what users can do.

Data can also be too hard to use. If data lacks clear labels or structure, it is confusing. People may struggle to understand maps or plan for their communities.

Finally, using raw data takes effort. We must have the skills to read it. If we lack these skills, we cannot use the information. This limits who can benefit from open data.
