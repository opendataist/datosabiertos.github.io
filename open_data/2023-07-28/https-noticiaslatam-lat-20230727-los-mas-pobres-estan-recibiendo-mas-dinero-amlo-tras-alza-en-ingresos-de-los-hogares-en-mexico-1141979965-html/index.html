The Mexican government highlights rising household incomes as evidence that social programs are successfully directing resources to the poorest citizens. This narrative contrasts with independent analyses indicating that a significant majority of the population still lacks sufficient income to escape poverty. The official celebration of these economic gains serves to legitimize the current administration's shift away from neoliberal policies toward a distinct model of "Mexican humanism." However, the data reveals that most employed individuals work in informal jobs without labor rights or social protection. This discrepancy between overall income averages and widespread underemployment suggests that structural vulnerabilities remain despite macroeconomic improvements. The findings challenge the assertion that poverty is being effectively solved, pointing instead to a labor market characterized by instability and lack of collective bargaining power. This article is relevant to open data because it illustrates how statistical releases can be subject to conflicting interpretations. While official surveys provide crucial metrics on income distribution, their impact depends on transparent access and critical analysis by civil society. Open data enables researchers to verify government claims, exposing gaps between reported progress and the lived reality of informal workers, thereby ensuring accountability in public policy evaluation.
