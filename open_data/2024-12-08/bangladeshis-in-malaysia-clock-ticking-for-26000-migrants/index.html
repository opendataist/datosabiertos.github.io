Bangladeshis in Malaysia: Clock ticking for 26,000 migrants

Bangladeshis in Malaysia: Clock ticking for 26,000 migrants

The article exposes systemic corruption within the Bangladesh High Commission in Malaysia, where officials colluded with touts to deliberately restrict e-passport issuance. By maintaining a shortage of machine-readable passports, this illicit network exploited migrant workers, charging excessive fees for services that were officially free or inexpensive. This manipulation highlights how opaque bureaucratic processes can be weaponized for financial gain, undermining the integrity of public service delivery. The situation underscores the critical importance of transparent, digital public data and service infrastructure. The transition to e-passports was intended to streamline processing and reduce human interference, yet a lack of clear communication and digital availability allowed corruption to persist. When essential civic information and processing statuses are not openly accessible, vulnerable populations become susceptible to exploitation by middlemen who thrive on information asymmetry and procedural opacity. This case is highly relevant to the open data movement as it demonstrates the real-world consequences of non-transparent governance. It illustrates that without open, accessible data regarding service availability, pricing, and processing times, citizens cannot hold institutions accountable. Promoting open data standards in diplomatic and consular services would mitigate such abuses, ensuring that migrant workers can verify legitimate processes and protect their rights against fraudulent actors.
