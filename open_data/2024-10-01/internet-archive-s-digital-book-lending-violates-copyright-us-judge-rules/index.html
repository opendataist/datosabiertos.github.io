Internet Archive's digital book lending violates copyright, US judge rules

A U.S. judge ruled that the Internet Archive infringed copyright by lending digital scans of protected books, rejecting its "controlled digital lending" and "fair use" arguments. The court determined that scanning and bulk digital distribution lacks the necessary transformation to qualify for fair use, as it effectively replaces licensed library copies with unauthorized digital versions. This decision reinforces the exclusive rights of copyright holders over their works, prioritizing the interests of authors and publishers in the digital marketplace. This ruling is critical to open data and open access movements because it sets a legal precedent that may restrict the digitization and free sharing of cultural heritage materials. By limiting the Internet Archive’s ability to preserve and provide access to copyrighted texts, the decision suggests that digital openness can be legally curtailed by traditional copyright enforcement. This creates a potential barrier for non-profit efforts aimed at democratizing knowledge and ensuring universal access to information. Consequently, the case highlights the ongoing tension between preserving public access to information and enforcing strict intellectual property protections. The Internet Archive’s intent to appeal indicates that this legal boundary is not yet fixed, leaving the future of open digital archives uncertain. For advocates of open knowledge, the outcome will significantly impact how digital libraries can operate, potentially forcing a reevaluation of strategies for preserving and sharing copyrighted content in the public interest.
