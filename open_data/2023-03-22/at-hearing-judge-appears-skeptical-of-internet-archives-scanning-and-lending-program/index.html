At Hearing, Judge Appears Skeptical of Internet Archive’s Scanning and Lending Program

The recent court hearing regarding the Internet Archive’s Controlled Digital Lending program reveals a significant legal challenge for digital preservation. Judge Koeltl’s skepticism highlights a critical distinction in copyright law: while libraries traditionally own the right to lend physical books they possess, this does not automatically grant them the authority to create unauthorized digital copies. The core legal battle hinges on whether mass digitization and subsequent digital lending constitute fair use or illegal infringement, with the court questioning the extent to which physical ownership extends to digital reproduction rights. From the perspective of open data and public access, this case establishes a precarious precedent for how information can be shared in the digital age. Publishers argue that the Internet Archive’s model undermines the economic viability of licensed e-book markets by replacing paid licenses with free, digitized alternatives. This tension underscores the fragility of non-commercial digital lending initiatives that rely on fair use defenses, suggesting that without clear legal protection, efforts to expand public access to copyrighted materials through digitization may be deemed unlawful market substitutes. The outcome of this litigation will likely dictate the future sustainability of open access archives. If the court rules against the Internet Archive, it signals that digital reproduction remains strictly controlled by copyright holders, limiting the ability of institutions to digitize and lend physical works freely. Conversely, a victory for the Archive would validate transformative digital lending as a lawful extension of traditional library services. Ultimately, this ruling determines whether open data ecosystems can rely on fair use to maintain broad, equitable access to cultural heritage, or if they must operate within restrictive, licensed frameworks.
