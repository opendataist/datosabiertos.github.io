Governments are becoming increasingly secretive. Here’s how they can be made to be more transparent

The article highlights a troubling decline in government transparency in Australia, characterized by dropping compliance with Senate document requests and a sharp decrease in successful Freedom of Information disclosures. This retreat from openness undermines democratic accountability, particularly for citizens without direct access to policymakers. The executive branch increasingly relies on "public interest immunity" claims to withhold information, creating a power imbalance where the government alone determines what remains secret. This lack of external oversight fosters public cynicism and suggests that transparency regimes are being actively undermined rather than protected. For open data advocates, these findings illustrate the critical need for robust, enforceable mechanisms that prevent governments from arbitrarily restricting data access. The significant rise in flawed initial FOI decisions and extended processing times demonstrates that without independent arbitration and adequate resourcing, transparency laws become ineffective tools rather than genuine obligations. The erosion of compliance rates signals that voluntary governmental cooperation is insufficient; structural reforms are required to ensure that data release is the norm, not the exception, and that citizens can trust the availability of public information. To address this crisis, the author recommends establishing independent legal arbiters to review immunity claims and strengthening parliamentary enforcement powers. Improving FOI outcomes also requires greater resource allocation for training and stricter legislative clarity to prevent administrative obfuscation. These changes are essential to rebalance power between the state and the public, ensuring that transparency serves as a genuine safeguard for democracy rather than a discretionary privilege controlled by those in power.
