Internet Archive's digital book lending violates copyright, US judge rules

Internet Archive's digital book lending violates copyright, US judge rules

A U.S. court ruled that the Internet Archive’s program of scanning copyrighted books to lend digital copies infringes on publishers' rights. The judge determined that this "controlled digital lending" did not qualify as fair use because the digital loans effectively replaced authorized licensing for traditional libraries. This decision establishes a critical legal boundary, preventing non-profits from bypassing copyright protections by digitizing physical collections they legally own. The ruling has significant implications for the open data and digital accessibility communities, as it challenges the feasibility of mass digitization initiatives. It signals that expanding access to copyrighted materials through free digital platforms may face substantial legal hurdles, potentially limiting the scope of open repositories. Consequently, organizations must reconsider how they balance universal access with strict intellectual property compliance. This case is relevant to open data because it highlights the tension between open access principles and existing copyright frameworks. It serves as a cautionary tale for data stewards and libraries, emphasizing that open distribution does not automatically equate to legal immunity. Understanding these limitations is crucial for developing sustainable models that respect creator rights while striving for broader information availability.
