The article illustrates a persistent conflict in Virginia between the legal presumption of public access under the Freedom of Information Act and the practical reality of officials treating government records as inherently private. By highlighting instances where leaders actively resist transparency, the piece underscores how bureaucratic habits often contradict statutory obligations, creating systemic barriers that prevent citizens from understanding how their government operates. Specific cases reveal the tangible consequences of this opacity. In one instance, withheld dispatch records obscured the context of a fatal police shooting, while in another, administrative negligence prevented business owners from reclaiming tax overpayments due to internal directives excluding such data from reports. These examples demonstrate that when information is concealed or mishandled, it not only deprives the public of essential insights but can also lead to unjust financial losses and hinder accountability in critical public safety matters. This narrative is directly relevant to open data advocates because it exposes the gap between policy and practice. It serves as a cautionary tale that without rigorous enforcement and cultural change, open government laws remain ineffective. The focus must shift from merely having transparency statutes to ensuring that data is proactively accessible, accurate, and complete, thereby empowering citizens to hold institutions accountable for their actions and decisions.
