'I didn’t realise I was being controlled' - type of abuse on the rise in Bradford

The article reveals a significant disparity in the criminal justice system’s response to coercive control, highlighting that despite a substantial rise in arrests, only a small fraction result in charges. This low conviction rate underscores the complex, intangible nature of psychological abuse, which often lacks the immediate physical evidence associated with traditional domestic violence. The data suggests that while law enforcement is increasingly proactive in identifying these patterns, the path from arrest to prosecution remains fraught with evidentiary challenges, leaving many victims without legal closure. Legislative changes, such as the Serious Crime Act 2015 and subsequent amendments, have expanded the definition of coercive control to include separated partners and removed residency requirements. These legal shifts have driven a sharp increase in reported incidents, reflecting greater societal awareness and broader police training rather than necessarily a surge in abuse itself. However, the narrative emphasizes that increased reporting does not equate to increased justice, as victims often face systemic barriers and isolation that complicate the judicial process and hinder effective accountability for perpetrators. This content is critically relevant to open data because it demonstrates how transparent access to police statistics exposes gaps in justice delivery and informs policy evaluation. By making arrest and charge rates public, open data allows civil society organizations to advocate for better investigative practices and victim support resources. It transforms raw numbers into actionable insights, enabling a deeper understanding of how legislative changes impact real-world outcomes and highlighting the urgent need for refined data collection to address the unique challenges of non-physical domestic abuse.
