Internet Archive's digital book lending violates copyright, US judge rules

Internet Archive's digital book lending violates copyright, US judge rules

A U.S. judge ruled that the Internet Archive infringed copyright by scanning and digitally lending books from major publishers, rejecting its claim of "fair use." The decision establishes that while physical lending is permitted, bulk scanning and digital distribution replace authorized publisher licenses, thereby violating copyright protections. This legal boundary clarifies that digital expansion of library services does not automatically inherit the exemptions granted to traditional physical lending. This ruling carries significant implications for open data and digital archiving efforts, as it challenges the scalability of non-profit digital repositories. It highlights the tension between preserving digital access to knowledge and respecting intellectual property rights in the modern information ecosystem. Organizations aiming to provide free, widespread access to cultural materials must now navigate stricter legal constraints regarding reproduction and distribution methods. The case underscores the ongoing negotiation between open access initiatives and commercial publishing interests. It suggests that future efforts to democratize information may require alternative models that do not rely on unauthorized scanning of copyrighted works. For the open data community, this serves as a critical reminder that accessibility goals must be balanced with legal compliance to ensure sustainable and lawful dissemination of knowledge resources.
