Baseless & Unidentified

Baseless & Unidentified

Nicholson Baker’s *Baseless* illustrates the profound challenges citizens face when attempting to access government records under the Freedom of Information Act. By documenting his decade-long struggle to retrieve documents regarding potential US biological weapons use in the Korean War, Baker reveals how bureaucratic delays, pervasive redactions, and systemic opacity obstruct historical transparency. The narrative highlights the tension between the public’s right to know and the state’s determination to maintain secrecy, showcasing the human cost of waiting for information that may never fully materialize. Colin Dickey’s *The Unidentified* offers a contrasting perspective on how society processes the unknown. Rather than seeking factual clarity, modern culture increasingly gravitates toward fringe beliefs and mythical narratives as a means to restore a sense of wonder in an increasingly rationalized world. Dickey argues that as traditional mysteries vanish, new ones are invented to fill the void, demonstrating that the human desire for the unexplained persists regardless of scientific advancement. This obsession reflects a deeper psychological need to find meaning beyond established empirical explanations. These works are highly relevant to open data initiatives because they expose the critical gaps between policy and reality. They underscore that providing data access is insufficient without addressing the cultural, bureaucratic, and structural barriers that prevent meaningful engagement with information. For open data advocates, these narratives serve as a powerful reminder that true transparency requires not just the release of records, but a proactive effort to overcome institutional inertia and restore public trust in government accountability.
