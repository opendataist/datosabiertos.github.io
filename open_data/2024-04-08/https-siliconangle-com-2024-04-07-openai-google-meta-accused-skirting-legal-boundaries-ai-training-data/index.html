The article critiques a New York Times report alleging that major tech companies like OpenAI, Google, and Meta engaged in questionable data scraping practices to train their AI models. Rather than accepting these claims as definitive proof of copyright infringement, the author argues that the report constructs a misleading narrative of "wholesale theft" by highlighting internal discussions about legal compliance. These conversations are presented not as evidence of wrongdoing, but as standard due diligence in an evolving legal landscape. A central theme is the ambiguity surrounding fair use laws regarding publicly available data used for AI training. The author points out the irony of the New York Times, which is currently suing OpenAI over these exact issues, publishing a report that paints the defendants negatively without direct proof of illegality. The text suggests that the legal framework is still gray, and AI companies are legitimately debating how to navigate these uncertainties while pursuing technological advancement, a process that should be viewed as normal industry discourse rather than malicious intent. This issue is highly relevant to open data because it touches on the fundamental tension between restrictive copyright enforcement and the open accessibility of data required to innovate. If internal deliberations about legality are criminalized or portrayed as unethical, it creates a chilling effect on data transparency and sharing. The article underscores the need for clear, stable regulations that balance creator rights with the open data principles essential for the development and democratization of artificial intelligence technologies.
