Violencia roba a niños hasta 50 clases por ciclo escolar

The article highlights a severe crisis in Mexican education, where organized violence and insecurity have forced the suspension of classes for significant portions of the school year. This disruption deprives students of a substantial share of their instructional time, compounding the direct loss of academic content with long-term psychological trauma and increased vulnerability to criminal recruitment. The phenomenon is not merely an isolated incident but a widespread structural issue that severely compromises the continuity and quality of education in affected regions. Transparency laws, central to the open data movement, have proven ineffective in exposing the full scale of this problem. Many state authorities failed to provide data, citing incompetence or technical barriers, while others claimed zero incidents, suggesting significant underreporting or a lack of systematic tracking. This opacity prevents a comprehensive understanding of how violence impacts education across the country, highlighting a critical gap in government accountability and public oversight. This case is highly relevant to open data because it demonstrates how the absence of accessible, standardized, and complete datasets hinders the ability to analyze and address social crises. When essential information about educational disruptions remains hidden or fragmented, policymakers and researchers cannot accurately assess the severity of the issue or design effective interventions. True transparency is necessary to expose these disparities and hold institutions accountable for protecting citizens' right to education.
