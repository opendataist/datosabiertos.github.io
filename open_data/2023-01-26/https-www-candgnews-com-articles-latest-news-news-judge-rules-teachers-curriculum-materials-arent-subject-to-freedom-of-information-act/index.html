A recent Michigan court ruling determined that public school teachers are not considered "public bodies" under the state’s Freedom of Information Act, significantly narrowing the scope of records accessible to citizens. The decision, which stemmed from a lawsuit over curriculum materials, asserts that teachers’ records do not qualify as public records simply because they are employees of the district. This legal interpretation suggests that schools have no obligation to actively seek out or produce instructional materials that are not already in their direct possession, effectively shielding classroom resources from immediate public scrutiny. The implications of this ruling extend far beyond individual teachers, potentially allowing various local government entities to evade transparency obligations by classifying documents held by lower-level employees as non-public. If upheld, this precedent could make it considerably harder for journalists, parents, and the general public to obtain information from local authorities, as these groups might hide documents behind the status of individual workers rather than the entity itself. This creates a troubling gap in government accountability, where the definition of a "public body" becomes so restrictive that core functions of local governance become opaque. This case is highly relevant to open data because it challenges the fundamental assumption that digital and educational records generated by public servants should be inherently accessible. It highlights a critical legal risk where the digitization of public services might coincide with a regression in transparency, allowing institutions to claim that data held by individuals does not constitute public information. For advocates of open data, this ruling underscores the urgent need to clarify legal definitions to ensure that all records created in the performance of public duties remain open, preventing a systemic erosion of citizen access to government information.
