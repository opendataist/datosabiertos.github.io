Hachette v. Internet Archive: All Our Coverage

The legal battle between major publishers and the Internet Archive has concluded without a Supreme Court review, cementing a significant precedent against "controlled digital lending." An appeals court upheld the lower court’s ruling that scanning and lending copyrighted print books violates copyright law, explicitly rejecting the Internet Archive’s fair use defense. This decision establishes that libraries cannot bypass traditional ownership limits by creating digital copies for lending, thereby prioritizing publisher rights over expansive digital access models proposed by digital archives. The case highlights a fundamental tension between traditional library lending principles and the realities of the digital publishing market. While the Internet Archive argued that its program was a modern adaptation of the first-sale doctrine essential for public access, the courts determined that unlicensed digital distribution harms the publishers’ potential market. By dismantling the legal framework for controlled digital lending, the ruling suggests that existing copyright laws do not currently support large-scale digitization of commercial works by non-commercial entities without permission. This outcome is critical for open data and digital preservation communities because it severely restricts the ability of archives to expand their collections beyond physically owned items. It signals that innovative open access initiatives relying on scanning user-owned media may face heightened legal scrutiny and risk. For those advocating for open access to knowledge, this case serves as a stark warning that technical infrastructure alone cannot override copyright constraints, necessitating more collaborative legal frameworks or alternative models for preserving and sharing digital cultural heritage.
