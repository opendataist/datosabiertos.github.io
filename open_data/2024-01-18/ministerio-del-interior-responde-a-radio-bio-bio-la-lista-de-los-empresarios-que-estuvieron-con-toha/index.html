Ministerio del Interior responde a Radio Bío Bío: la lista de los empresarios que estuvieron con Tohá

The core issue centers on the interpretation of lobbying regulations, specifically whether a meeting involving high-level government officials and private sector leaders constitutes a registered lobbying activity. The Interior Minister initially resisted disclosing attendees, arguing that the event lacked the formal elements of lobbying and therefore should not be recorded in the official transparency registry. This stance highlights a critical ambiguity in defining legitimate political consultation versus regulated influence peddling, creating a gray area where ministers can decline transparency obligations based on subjective assessments of intent. Ultimately, the obligation to reveal participant identities was fulfilled only after a formal request under the Transparency Law, forcing the government to publish a list of prominent business figures. This outcome underscores the indispensable role of public interest litigation and legal frameworks in enforcing accountability when political entities attempt to limit disclosure. The delay and initial refusal demonstrate that without proactive legal pressure, information regarding elite access to decision-makers may remain hidden, compromising the principle of open governance. This case is highly relevant to open data because it illustrates the tension between voluntary transparency and mandatory data publication. It reveals how the classification of interactions—such as defining a conversation as mere political dialogue rather than lobbying—can determine whether specific datasets are generated or withheld. For open data advocates, this emphasizes the necessity of precise, legally binding definitions of "lobbying" to ensure that all significant exchanges between the public sector and private interests are captured in accessible, standardized registers, preventing the erosion of public trust through selective opacity.
