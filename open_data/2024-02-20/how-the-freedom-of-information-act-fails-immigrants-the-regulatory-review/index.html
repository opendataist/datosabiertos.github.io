How the Freedom of Information Act Fails Immigrants | The Regulatory Review

Current immigration proceedings deny noncitizens formal discovery rights, creating a severe information asymmetry that threatens due process. Instead of automatically accessing evidence, immigrants must rely on the Freedom of Information Act to retrieve their records, a mechanism originally intended for public oversight rather than individual legal defense. This structural flaw often prevents individuals from effectively challenging deportation orders or proving their legal status, as the burden of proof is placed disproportionately on the accused. The FOIA system is ill-equipped to handle the urgent needs of immigration cases due to significant procedural delays and opaque withholding practices. Response times frequently exceed legal limits, often arriving too late to influence hearing outcomes, while agencies may use exemptions to withhold or redact critical evidence. Furthermore, complex bureaucratic hurdles and legal doctrines can further restrict access, effectively allowing the government to use secret evidence against immigrants without adequate challenge, thereby undermining the fairness of the adjudication process. This issue is critical to open data advocacy as it highlights the gap between transparency laws and individual rights in sensitive administrative contexts. Reforming this system requires digitizing paper records and centralizing storage to improve accessibility and speed, moving beyond manual retrieval bottlenecks. By establishing expedited, dedicated systems for immigration records, the government can leverage data infrastructure to uphold due process, demonstrating how open data principles should evolve to protect vulnerable populations rather than just facilitating general public scrutiny.
