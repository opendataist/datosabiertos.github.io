The Government's Antipathy Towards Transparency Has Made FOIA Lawsuits The Default Process

The article argues that the Freedom of Information Act has been effectively neutralized by agencies that routinely ignore requests, forcing citizens to file lawsuits just to access basic public records. Rather than proactively ensuring transparency, these government bodies treat judicial mandates as the standard operating procedure for disclosure. This shift transforms FOIA from a tool of immediate accountability into a slow, costly legal battle, undermining the legislative intent of open governance and hiding significant operational details from public scrutiny. This systemic failure is particularly concerning for law enforcement and counterterrorism agencies, which actively resist requests for information regarding their interior enforcement practices and surveillance technologies. By stalling or ignoring inquiries, these agencies operate with excessive opacity, preventing the public from understanding how their freedoms are impacted by inland policing and expanded surveillance capabilities. The reliance on litigation to uncover such vital information suggests a deliberate strategy to limit exposure, knowing that few individuals have the resources to fight for their right to know. The relevance to open data lies in the erosion of public trust and the obstruction of accountability mechanisms. When government data remains inaccessible without legal coercion, the fundamental promise of an open government fails. This narrative highlights the critical need for procedural reforms that prioritize voluntary compliance and proactive disclosure. Without ensuring that data flows freely to the public without barriers, the transparency essential for democratic oversight is compromised, leaving citizens powerless to hold their government accountable.
