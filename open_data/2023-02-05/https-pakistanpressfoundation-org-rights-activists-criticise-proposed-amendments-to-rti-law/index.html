Proposed amendments to Pakistan’s Right of Access to Information Act threaten to exclude Parliament from the definition of public bodies, effectively shielding legislative activities from citizen scrutiny. This legislative shift aims to prevent the National Assembly and Senate from being compelled to disclose information, a move that rights activists condemn as a significant regression in democratic accountability. By attempting to remove these core institutions from the law's purview, the bill undermines the fundamental principle that elected representatives must remain answerable to the public they serve. Such exclusions violate constitutional guarantees for information access, potentially setting a dangerous precedent where all major state institutions seek similar blanket exemptions. Activists argue that transparency strengthens parliamentary sanctity, whereas secrecy erodes trust and diminishes civic participation. If parliament escapes these obligations, other powerful bodies may follow suit, leading to a systemic collapse in government openness. This retrogressive step ignores previous commissions’ orders for disclosure, turning earlier gains in governance transparency into irreversible losses for civil society. This issue is critical to open data advocates because it highlights the vulnerability of legal frameworks protecting public information against political interference. It underscores the necessity of robust, inclusive legislation that ensures all state organs, including legislatures, are bound by transparency mandates. The fight against these amendments serves as a vital reminder that open data is not merely a technical standard but a constitutional right essential for preventing corruption and ensuring governmental accountability. Protecting these laws is fundamental to maintaining an informed citizenry.
