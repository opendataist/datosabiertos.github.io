Texas’ open records law is 50 years old — and routinely flouted

Texas’ Public Information Act, designed to ensure government transparency, is increasingly undermined by systemic delays and intentional obfuscation. Agencies routinely exploit loopholes, such as delaying responses by seeking attorney general opinions or withholding court records under the guise of processing time. This creates an "obstacle course" that discourages public access and fuels skepticism, as officials often treat the law as a weapon rather than a tool for accountability. The lack of meaningful enforcement allows agencies to ignore requests with impunity, knowing that litigation is costly and rarely pursued by citizens. While bipartisan consensus exists that current exceptions are too broad, proposed reforms like empowering local judges to expedite decisions have stalled. Consequently, the presumption of openness is replaced by suspicion, as the government holds disproportionate power and resources compared to individual requesters. This article is critical for open data advocates because it highlights how procedural barriers, rather than just technical limitations, erode the value of public information. It demonstrates that without robust enforcement and streamlined processes, open data laws fail to deliver timely, usable insights. The situation underscores the urgent need for structural changes that prioritize immediate accessibility and hold agencies accountable for withholding data, ensuring transparency translates into genuine public trust.
