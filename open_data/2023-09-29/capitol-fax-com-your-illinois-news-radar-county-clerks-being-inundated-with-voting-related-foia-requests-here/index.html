The discussion highlights the growing tension between legitimate public transparency and the abusive use of Freedom of Information Act requests. Commenters argue that while open government data is essential for democracy, these legal mechanisms are frequently exploited to harass election officials, clog administrative systems, and fund political grift rather than uncover genuine fraud. The prevailing view suggests that such tactics are often driven by bad-faith actors seeking to undermine trust in electoral processes through deliberate obstruction and misleading narratives. A central practical challenge identified is the disconnect between public expectations and technical reality regarding data formats. Government records are rarely stored in easily exportable or consolidated forms, making compliant responses burdensome, particularly for smaller agencies with limited resources. Many requests demand the creation of new data sets or the extraction of specific information that does not exist in a ready-to-use state, forcing officials to either deny clearly non-responsive demands or expend excessive effort on poorly defined queries. This phenomenon is highly relevant to open_data because it underscores the critical importance of standardization, machine-readability, and proactive data publication. If public records were available in standardized, accessible formats, it would significantly reduce the administrative burden on agencies and mitigate the potential for strategic misuse of information requests. Ultimately, the conversation calls for a shift from reactive compliance to proactive transparency, ensuring that data is structured in a way that supports genuine public inquiry while protecting against systemic harassment.
