Internet Archive’s digital library has been found in breach of copyright. What are the implications?

The court’s ruling against the Internet Archive clarifies that controlled digital lending of copyrighted works constitutes infringement, establishing that digital access must respect creators' rights rather than bypassing traditional sales channels. This decision underscores a critical distinction between non-profit preservation efforts and the unauthorized distribution of commercial content, affirming that authors and publishers retain the right to control and monetize their digital licenses. For open data and digital libraries, this highlights the necessity of balancing universal access with legal compliance, ensuring that digitization projects do not undermine existing commercial markets for authors. The legal gap left by the ruling, which allows the retention of non-digital physical books, poses a potential threat to future digital markets by enabling free access to works before they are officially published in ebook formats. This scenario creates a chilling effect on creators, as it allows institutions to preemptively release content for free, potentially disincentivizing the investment required to digitize out-of-print works. For open data initiatives, this implies that preservation strategies must be carefully structured to avoid competing with legitimate commercial availability, ensuring that open access does not erode the economic foundation of content creation. This case reinforces the principle that intellectual labor is not a public commodity and that sustainable open access models must compensate creators, particularly as digital lending expands globally. The expansion of lending rights to ebooks in jurisdictions like Australia demonstrates a growing recognition that digital access requires financial support for authors, aligning with broader efforts to protect creative industries from exploitation. Ultimately, the article illustrates that true openness in data and culture requires respecting the economic realities of creation, ensuring that free access does not come at the cost of stifling future innovation or depriving creators of their livelihood.
