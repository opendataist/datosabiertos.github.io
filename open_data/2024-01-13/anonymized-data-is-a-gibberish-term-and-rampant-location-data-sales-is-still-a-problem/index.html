'Anonymized Data' Is A Gibberish Term, And Rampant Location Data Sales Is Still A Problem

The article challenges the widespread assumption that data anonymization effectively protects user privacy, particularly regarding location information. Despite companies and government officials claiming that stripping personal identifiers makes data safe, experts demonstrate that location traces are highly re-identifiable. By combining geographic patterns with other available datasets, it is relatively easy to link "anonymous" location data back to specific individuals, rendering standard anonymization techniques insufficient for protecting personal identity. This misconception facilitates a opaque industry of data brokers who buy, sell, and trade location records with minimal transparency or user consent. Companies often deny selling access to this data while simultaneously profiting from its exchange, relying on public ignorance of their operations. The lack of clarity regarding data sources and usage prevents users from exercising true control over their digital footprints, creating an environment where privacy violations occur without adequate disclosure or accountability. The piece is critical for open data discussions because it highlights the dangerous gap between technical claims of privacy and the reality of data reuse. It underscores the urgent need for rigorous standards in data sharing and strict regulatory frameworks that recognize the limitations of anonymization. Without stronger federal privacy laws and oversight, the open data ecosystem risks perpetuating significant privacy harms, as the current lack of enforcement allows continuous exploitation of user information under the guise of anonymity.
