Internet Archive's digital book lending violates copyright, US judge rules

Internet Archive's digital book lending violates copyright, US judge rules

A U.S. judge ruled that the Internet Archive infringed copyright by digitally scanning and lending books from major publishers without permission. The court rejected the organization’s "fair use" defense, determining that creating digital copies to replace authorized library licenses lacks the necessary transformation. This decision clarifies that while physical books can be lent, bulk digital reproduction requires explicit licensing, significantly impacting how digital collections are managed. For open data advocates, this ruling highlights the delicate balance between preserving cultural heritage and respecting intellectual property rights. It demonstrates that digitization projects cannot bypass existing copyright frameworks simply by arguing for increased accessibility or educational value. Organizations relying on open access models must navigate strict legal boundaries to avoid infringing on creators' rights, ensuring that their efforts do not undermine the economic structures supporting content creation. The case underscores the growing tension between digital preservation initiatives and commercial publishing interests. As libraries and archives increasingly move toward digital formats, they face greater scrutiny regarding how they acquire and distribute materials. This precedent suggests that future open data projects will need robust legal strategies to ensure compliance, emphasizing that open access must coexist with, rather than undermine, established copyright protections.
