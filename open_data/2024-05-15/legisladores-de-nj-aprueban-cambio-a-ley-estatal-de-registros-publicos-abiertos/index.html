Legisladores de NJ aprueban cambio a ley estatal de registros públicos abiertos

New Jersey has approved a controversial reform to its public records law, known as OPRA, facing strong criticism from civil rights groups and the press. This update, the first in more than two decades, aims to ease the administrative burden on government employees amid the volume of requests, including those of a commercial nature. However, the measure has sparked intense debate over its real impact on institutional transparency. The legislation introduces significant changes, such as allowing companies to pay higher fees for the production of records and establishing mechanisms to take legal action against requesters deemed disruptive. Opponents argue that these modifications, combined with the elimination of the requirement for cities to cover attorneys’ fees in lost cases, will create insurmountable economic barriers. This could discourage the public and journalists from challenging the improper withholding of information, limiting their ability to hold the government accountable. This case is relevant to the open data community because it illustrates the ongoing tension between administrative efficiency and the fundamental right to access information. The reform demonstrates how regulations can indirectly affect the availability and accessibility of public data, even when the stated purpose is to support government agencies. The reduction in legal resources available to citizens sets a concerning precedent, suggesting that transparency could become an economic privilege rather than a universal right, thereby threatening the integrity and scrutiny of open data.
