Percepción de seguridad en la frontera

Percepción de seguridad en la frontera

The article demonstrates that perceived safety is as critical as crime statistics in evaluating urban well-being. It highlights Ciudad Juárez’s significant improvement in citizens’ perception of security, contrasting sharply with the worsening situation in the state capital, Chihuahua. This divergence underscores how specific local policies and police-community engagement directly influence public confidence. The narrative suggests that safety is not merely about law enforcement but involves proximity between authorities and citizens, affecting daily life in public spaces and transportation. This distinction is vital for open data initiatives, which often prioritize quantifiable metrics, such as reported crimes, over subjective perceptions. By focusing solely on hard numbers, data ecosystems may overlook the nuanced realities of how citizens experience their environment. The article argues that comprehensive safety analysis must integrate both objective incidents and subjective feelings to provide a holistic view of urban health, thereby preventing misleading conclusions derived from incomplete datasets. Understanding the link between security, mobility, and economic potential reveals broader implications for development. A city perceived as safe attracts investment and fosters social progress, making transparency in these perceptions essential. For open data, this means advocating for platforms that publish not only crime rates but also surveys on citizen sentiment. Such holistic data empowers communities to demand better services and helps policymakers address the root causes of insecurity, fostering trust through visible, accountable governance.
