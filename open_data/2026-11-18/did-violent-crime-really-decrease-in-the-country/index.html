Did violent crime really decrease in the country?

Did violent crime really decrease in the country?

The article questions the validity of the Mexican government’s official figures on the reduction of homicides, highlighting a significant discrepancy with data from the National Institute of Statistics and Geography (INEGI). While the administration boasts of a sharp decline based on records from prosecutors’ offices, civil statistics reflect a notably higher number of deaths from lethal violence. This divergence suggests that the public safety narrative may be distorted by selective methodologies that ignore the full reality of violence in the country. Experts and researchers point out that this difference stems from questionable practices such as the reclassification of crimes, in which violent deaths are categorized as accidental or as other types of offenses to artificially inflate the drop in intentional homicides. Moreover, the rise in missing persons cases is interpreted as an indicator that many unlocated bodies are being excluded from homicide statistics, thereby concealing the true scale of violence and the impact of organized crime in specific territories. This situation is relevant to open data because it illustrates how a lack of transparency and inconsistencies among official sources hinder public scrutiny. The absence of a unified and auditable methodology prevents the generation of reliable data, which is essential for evidence-based policymaking. The case underscores the urgency of ensuring that state statistics are comprehensive, comparable, and free from manipulation, in order to promote accountability and statistical truth.
