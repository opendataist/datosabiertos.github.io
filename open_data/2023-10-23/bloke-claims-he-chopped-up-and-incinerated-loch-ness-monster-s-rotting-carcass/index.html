Bloke claims he chopped up and incinerated Loch Ness Monster's rotting carcass

This article challenges the persistent myth of the Loch Ness Monster by citing historical records and official council responses indicating that a large, unidentified carcass was discovered and disposed of in 1942. According to these findings, a local surveyor found the remains, which bore a striking resemblance to classic descriptions of the creature, and subsequently incinerated and buried them at a site that now underlies a school. The decision to destroy the evidence was reportedly influenced by wartime security concerns, as the area was a critical naval base, leading to a long period of silence surrounding the incident. The revelation suggests that the enduring mystery is likely a historical misunderstanding rather than an ongoing biological phenomenon. While modern searches continue to yield no evidence of the beast, this account provides a concrete explanation for the lack of recent sightings. It implies that the "monster" was a rare straggler or distinct entity that was removed from the ecosystem decades ago, effectively ending the era of credible sightings. The narrative shifts the focus from active hunting to historical curiosity, offering a closure to a century-old legend based on municipal records rather than eyewitness speculation. This story is relevant to the open data movement because it demonstrates the power of Freedom of Information requests in uncovering hidden truths. By requesting specific records from local government, a private citizen was able to validate a fringe historical claim that official narratives had largely ignored. It highlights how transparent access to public records can dismantle myths, preserve local history, and empower citizens to hold institutions accountable for their past actions. Furthermore, it underscores the importance of preserving digitized historical documents, as they serve as vital evidence for contemporary investigations into legacy issues.
