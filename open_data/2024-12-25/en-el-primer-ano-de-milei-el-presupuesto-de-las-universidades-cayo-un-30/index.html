En el primer año de Milei, el presupuesto de las universidades cayó un 30%

En el primer año de Milei, el presupuesto de las universidades cayó un 30%

The article describes the tension between the national government and the public university sector, evidenced by a drastic budget cut that negatively impacted faculty salaries. This situation triggered a massive mobilization of students and academics that, despite enjoying broad social support, faced resistance from the Executive Branch, which justified its measures on the premise of demanding greater transparency and auditing of educational institutions, denying the rectors' accusations of opacity. The relevance of this case for *open data* lies in the central debate over the accessibility and clarity of public information. While the government argues that universities must undergo rigorous scrutiny to ensure spending efficiency, the academic community maintains that transparency already exists through public systems and independent auditors. This conflict underscores the critical need for budgetary and administrative data to be not only public but also understandable and verifiable by citizens, preventing the lack of clarity in information from being used as a political tool to discredit institutional management or to justify cuts to essential resources. Finally, the legislative outcome, with the maintenance of the veto on the funding bill, consolidates a gap between the social demand for autonomy and adequate financing and current fiscal policy. For the open data ecosystem, this implies that the struggle for institutional transparency is not limited to technical access to data but is intrinsically linked to civil society's capacity to interpret and use that information in defense of fundamental public services. The current crisis demonstrates that without robust and neutral data governance, information can become a field of political dispute that weakens accountability and the quality of higher education.
