Labor's Trumpian lies about robodebt are at the heart of its assault on freedom of information

The proposed Freedom of Information legislation represents a severe regression in government transparency, directly contradicting the Labor government’s narrative. Although Attorney-General Michelle Rowland claims the bill addresses the lack of accountability exposed by the robodebt scandal, it actually expands the cabinet document exemption rather than reforming it. This move undermines the Royal Commission’s recommendation to remove blanket confidentiality, effectively allowing ministers to hide deliberations under the guise of protecting cabinet processes. This expansion of secrecy is justified by unfounded claims regarding foreign interference and national security risks, arguments that lack empirical evidence. By widening the scope of exempt documents, the government is prioritizing political evasion over public accountability. This approach echoes historical attempts to shield decision-making from scrutiny, suggesting that the administration views transparency as an obstacle rather than a democratic necessity, thereby perpetuating the very opacity that allowed systemic failures like robodebt to persist. The article is critically relevant to open data advocates because it highlights the fragility of legal frameworks designed to release public sector information. When statutory protections are weakened, the availability of high-quality, unredacted government data diminishes, hindering independent analysis, investigative journalism, and public oversight. Understanding this political context is essential for activists who rely on robust FOI laws to access the raw data needed to challenge official narratives and ensure governmental integrity.
