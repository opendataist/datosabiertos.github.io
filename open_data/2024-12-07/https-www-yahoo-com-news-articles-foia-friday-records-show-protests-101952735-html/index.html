The article highlights a persistent disconnect in Virginia between legal mandates for public access and the practical reality of government secrecy. Despite the Freedom of Information Act presuming records are public, officials frequently treat information as private by default, requiring citizens to justify why data should be shared. This systemic resistance undermines the transparency laws designed to hold power accountable, creating an environment where closed-door decision-making remains the norm rather than the exception. Several specific cases illustrate how this opacity suppresses public scrutiny. University officials concealed policy discussions regarding student protests, while former corrupt police officers retained pensions due to unclear legal provisions. Furthermore, recent legislative changes allowed disciplinary hearings for law enforcement to occur behind closed doors, limiting accountability for misconduct. These instances demonstrate that when access is restricted, the public cannot effectively evaluate governance or challenge decisions that impact civil liberties and public safety. This narrative is crucial to the open data movement because it reveals that technical access is insufficient without cultural and legal compliance. True openness requires not just available datasets, but a commitment from institutions to proactively release information. Without robust transparency, data silos prevent citizens from understanding how decisions are made, eroding trust and hindering the ability to engage meaningfully in democratic processes.
