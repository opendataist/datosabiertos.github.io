This incident underscores the tension between data protection and public accountability in the open data sphere. When organizations cite small community sizes or indirect identifiability as reasons to deny freedom of information requests, they risk hiding systemic institutional failures. The lack of transparent records prevents the community and regulators from recognizing dangerous trends before they escalate into serious criminal acts. Relevance to open data lies in the urgent need to balance legitimate privacy concerns with the necessity of transparency in public service. The case suggests that current data sharing practices may be too restrictive, effectively shielding negligent institutions. To prevent future harm, there must be clearer, more robust frameworks for releasing anonymized yet meaningful data on professional conduct, ensuring that accountability is not sacrificed under the guise of protecting individual identities in small networks.
