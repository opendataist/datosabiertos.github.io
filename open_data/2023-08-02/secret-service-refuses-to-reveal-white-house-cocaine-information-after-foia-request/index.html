Secret Service Refuses to Reveal White House Cocaine Information after FOIA Request

The U.S. Secret Service denied a Freedom of Information Act request regarding an investigation into cocaine found in the White House, asserting that visitor records are governed by the Presidential Records Act rather than FOIA. This legal maneuver highlights a significant tension between government transparency laws and executive branch privacy protections. The agencies argue that such data remains under the exclusive control of the White House, thereby shielding it from standard public records requests initiated by external organizations like the Heritage Foundation. Legal experts suggest this distinction creates a loophole that may prevent accountability if the Secret Service merely consulted existing logs without creating new, distinct agency records. If no new documents were generated by the investigators, the refusal to release information implies a lack of thorough inquiry. Conversely, if independent records were created, they should technically be accessible under FOIA. This ambiguity raises serious questions about whether the investigation was conducted with sufficient rigor or if it was intentionally sidelined to protect specific individuals. This situation is crucial to the open data community because it illustrates how statutory classifications can be used to obscure public accountability. It underscores the need for clear definitions of what constitutes an "agency record" versus a "presidential record" when national security or high-profile investigations are involved. Without strict adherence to open data principles, critical information about government activities and potential misconduct can remain inaccessible, undermining public trust and the fundamental right to know.
