Seaton highlights costs of Augusta County supervisors FOIA lawsuit, attempts to stop appeal

The article highlights a significant conflict within the Augusta County Board of Supervisors regarding the financial and ethical implications of appealing a court ruling that ordered the release of a closed-session recording. Supervisor Scott Seaton argues that spending taxpayer money to defend an illegal closed meeting is unjustified, especially given the lack of certainty that the appeal will succeed in overturning established state precedents. This situation underscores the tension between government transparency and the administrative desire to maintain privacy, illustrating how public entities can inadvertently prioritize self-protection over accountability. The core issue revolves around the interpretation of open meeting laws, specifically the requirement for specific justification when entering closed sessions. A circuit court judge previously ruled that the county’s vague citation was insufficient under recent Virginia Supreme Court precedents, mandating greater particularity. Seaton’s attempt to halt the appeal emphasizes the need for public entities to adhere strictly to these transparency standards, suggesting that the cost of defending non-compliance outweighs any potential benefit to the supervisors themselves. This case is highly relevant to open data and transparency advocates as it demonstrates the real-world costs associated with obstructing public access to government records. It illustrates how resistance to FOIA requests can lead to significant legal expenditures without guaranteeing privacy, thereby wasting public resources. The narrative serves as a cautionary tale for local governments, reinforcing the importance of proactive compliance with open data principles to avoid costly legal battles and maintain public trust.
