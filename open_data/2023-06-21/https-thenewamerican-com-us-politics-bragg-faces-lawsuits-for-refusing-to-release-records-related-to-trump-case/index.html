The article highlights a growing crisis of transparency in the American justice system, specifically focusing on Manhattan District Attorney Alvin Bragg’s refusal to comply with Freedom of Information Law requests regarding communications with federal officials and Democratic politicians. By withholding records that could reveal potential coordination in the prosecution of Donald Trump, Bragg’s office is accused of operating under a dual standard that prioritizes political outcomes over legal accountability. This lack of disclosure raises serious questions about the independence of local prosecutions and suggests that judicial processes may be influenced by partisan interests rather than objective legal merit. Parallel to these transparency concerns, the recent plea deal granted to Hunter Biden is presented as evidence of systemic inequality, contrasting sharply with the extensive legal challenges facing the Trump family. Critics argue that while Trump faces rigorous prosecution, significant allegations against the Biden administration’s first family were dismissed or resolved through lenient agreements. This disparity fuels public skepticism and reinforces the narrative that the justice system is weaponized against political opponents, eroding trust in government institutions and suggesting that high-ranking officials are exempt from the same scrutiny applied to ordinary citizens. This situation is highly relevant to open data advocates because it underscores the critical necessity of accessible, unredacted government records to verify integrity and prevent abuse of power. When agencies claim they lack the systems to produce basic documents, it obstructs public oversight and hides potential misconduct behind bureaucratic excuses. True accountability requires that data regarding inter-agency communications and prosecutorial decisions be freely available, allowing citizens and watchdogs to detect bias and ensure that legal actions are based on facts rather than political convenience.
