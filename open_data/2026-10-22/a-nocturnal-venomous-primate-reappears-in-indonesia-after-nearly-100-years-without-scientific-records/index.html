A nocturnal, venomous primate reappears in Indonesia after nearly 100 years without scientific records

A nocturnal, venomous primate reappears in Indonesia after nearly 100 years without scientific records

The discovery of more than ninety specimens of the Bangka slow loris in Indonesia refutes the belief that this species might be extinct after decades without scientific records. This finding, based on rescue data from the past decade, confirms that the primate survives in various areas of the island, challenging previous IUCN listings that considered them lost. The survival of the species is primarily threatened by habitat loss resulting from mining and agriculture, as well as direct risks such as electrocution. Despite these adversities, conservation efforts have enabled the successful release of rescued animals, demonstrating that human impact can be mitigated when action is taken with determination and local knowledge. This case is crucial for open data in conservation, as it illustrates how systematic documentation of incidents and rescues can generate vital evidence about biodiversity. The availability of these records allows the scientific community to update distribution models, identify critical areas, and guide public policy, demonstrating that transparency in data is key to saving forgotten species.
