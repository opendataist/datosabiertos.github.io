Internet Archive's digital book lending violates copyright, US judge rules

A U.S. judge ruled that the Internet Archive infringed copyright by digitally scanning and lending protected books, rejecting its claim of fair use. The decision clarifies that while owning physical books allows for lending them, scanning copies to create digital versions does not qualify as transformative use under current law. This establishes a critical legal boundary for digital lending practices. For open data advocates, this ruling highlights the tension between expanding digital access and respecting intellectual property rights. It serves as a cautionary tale for projects aiming to digitize cultural heritage, demonstrating that simply making content freely available online may not protect against legal challenges. Organizations must carefully navigate copyright laws to ensure their preservation efforts remain sustainable and compliant. Ultimately, this case emphasizes the need for clear frameworks that balance the public’s right to information with creators’ rights. As open data initiatives grow, stakeholders must recognize that unrestricted digital distribution of copyrighted materials carries significant legal risks. Protecting access to knowledge requires innovative solutions that respect existing legal structures while fostering broader educational opportunities.
