'Anonymized Data' Is A Gibberish Term, And Rampant Location Data Sales Is Still A Problem

The widespread belief that anonymized personal data is safe is fundamentally flawed, as studies consistently demonstrate that it is trivial to re-identify individuals by cross-referencing limited data points with other available sources. This misconception is particularly dangerous regarding location data, which reveals intimate details about a person’s daily life, such as their home, workplace, and habits. Consequently, companies and government officials often rely on the false security of "anonymization" to justify the extensive collection and sale of this sensitive information without adequate oversight or user consent. This lack of transparency is exacerbated by a vast, opaque network of data brokers who operate with near-total secrecy to maintain the flow of location information. Major entities, including tech firms and wireless carriers, participate in this market while claiming their practices are transparent and user-controlled, yet they frequently obscure the origins of the data and the extent to which user apps contribute to these sales. The resulting environment allows these intermediaries to profit significantly from personal tracking while remaining largely immune to public scrutiny or accountability, as their business models depend on keeping the public unaware of the sheer scale of data exploitation. For the open data community, this article highlights the critical tension between data utility and privacy preservation, illustrating why simple de-identification is insufficient for ethical data practices. It underscores the urgent need for robust federal privacy laws and true transparency in data ecosystems, as current regulatory efforts are stalled by powerful industry lobbying. The persistent abuse of location data serves as a stark warning that without strict governance and genuine anonymization techniques that account for re-identification risks, open data initiatives risk perpetuating systemic privacy violations rather than fostering public trust and responsible information sharing.
