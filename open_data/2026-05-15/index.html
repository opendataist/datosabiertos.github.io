Paraguay’s transparency turns raw feminicide data into a force for justice

We often ignore words like “transparency” and “freedom.” We think they are just boring government talk. But these words are powerful tools for justice. In Paraguay, the Ministry of Public is using them to fight gender-based violence. They are turning raw numbers into a force for social change. This effort makes hidden problems visible. It allows communities to demand answers from those in power.

The Ministry released detailed data on feminicides. This data shows a hard truth: the most dangerous place for a woman is her own home. The numbers reveal that violence mostly happens in domestic settings. It often involves people who are close to the victim. The central regions of the country and the start of the year are hotspots for this violence. This transparency challenges a common myth. Many people think violence is mostly committed by strangers. The data proves otherwise. It shows that aggression from intimate partners is the main pattern. The initiative highlights the severe human cost. It even includes the indirect victims, such as children who witness these crimes.

Moving from stories to hard evidence makes public safety stronger. When government data is easy to access and use, citizens can understand the true size of a problem. This understanding is the first step toward good policies. For parents raising the next generation, seeing these patterns clearly is essential. It helps them advocate for targeted solutions. We can use this raw data to demand specific protections for vulnerable groups. We do not need to rely on vague guesses anymore.

However, having access to data is only half the battle. To use this information for justice, we must interpret it correctly. We must understand what the numbers mean in context. We cannot just look at a spike in figures. Before you quote or rely on these statistics to drive change, you must read them accurately and ethically. You can learn how to critically evaluate these figures in our guide on how to read a statistic before you quote it.

This transparency has deep consequences. Accessible government datasets empower communities. They expose hidden social realities. This approach shifts the burden of proof. It moves the focus from the victim to the system. This forces policymakers to address the root causes of violence. They must fix the problems, not just the symptoms. It turns passive observation into active participation. This allows us to hold institutions accountable. It drives systemic interventions that protect human rights.

The legal framework around this data ensures it remains a public good. The datasets released by the Ministry are not locked behind paywalls. They do not have restrictive copyrights. They are shared under licenses that allow reuse. This allows researchers, journalists, and citizens to analyze, remix, and share the information. This further supports the cause of transparency. Understanding what you are allowed to do with this information is key to maximizing its impact. For more details on the rights and responsibilities that come with using public data, see our guide on what licence open data carries, and what you may do with it.

This initiative in Paraguay shows that open data is a tool for democracy. It is not just a technical exercise in digital storage. It facilitates evidence-based decision-making. It promotes public accountability by revealing specific patterns of violence. These patterns were previously hidden. By making this data shared and accessible, we enable a collective response to injustice. We move from ignorance. We stop seeing violence as a series of bad accidents. We start recognizing it as a structural issue. This requires structural solutions. We leave a better world for our children by ensuring the truth is not just recorded. It must be understood, used, and acted upon.
