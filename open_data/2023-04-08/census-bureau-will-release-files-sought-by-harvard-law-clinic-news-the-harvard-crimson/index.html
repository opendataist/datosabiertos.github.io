Census Bureau Will Release Files Sought by Harvard Law Clinic | News | The Harvard Crimson

The U.S. Census Bureau has agreed to release the raw, unprocessed data files from the 2020 Census, marking a significant victory for academic researchers seeking to verify the integrity of redistricting data. After a prolonged legal battle initiated by Harvard Law School’s Election Law Clinic, this decision allows scholars to investigate whether privacy techniques employed during the census led to the underrepresentation of racial minorities. By granting access to these "noisy" measurements, the bureau enables independent verification of potential biases that could otherwise remain hidden within final, processed statistics. This transparency is crucial for ensuring accurate political representation and equitable federal funding distribution. Researchers can now apply statistical corrections to determine if specific groups, such as Native Americans in low-density areas, were systematically undercounted. Without these raw files, correcting for the artificial noise introduced by differential privacy is nearly impossible, potentially skewing apportionment results and undermining democratic processes. The ability to audit these methods directly addresses long-standing concerns about how data privacy measures might inadvertently harm marginalized communities’ political power. For the open data community, this case highlights the critical importance of preserving intermediate data artifacts to facilitate independent scrutiny. It demonstrates that releasing raw or partially processed data empowers the public and academia to hold institutions accountable for methodological choices. This precedent supports the broader open data movement by proving that transparency in government data pipelines is essential for trust, equity, and the accurate application of statistical science in public policy.
