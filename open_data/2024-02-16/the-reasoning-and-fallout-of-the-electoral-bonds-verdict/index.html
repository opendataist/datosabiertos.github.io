The reasoning and fallout of the electoral bonds verdict

The Supreme Court struck down the electoral bonds scheme, ruling that it violates citizens’ right to know under Article 19(1)(a). The Court emphasized that transparent information regarding political funding is essential for informed voter choice and participatory democracy. By granting selective anonymity to donors while excluding political parties, the scheme undermined political equality and enabled undue corporate influence, effectively allowing wealth to skew the electoral process through potential quid pro quo arrangements. Furthermore, the judgment clarified that informational privacy, recognized as a fundamental right, extends to political affiliation. The Court held that without privacy for political beliefs and financial contributions, citizens risk suppression, discrimination, and voter disenfranchisement. However, the specific anonymity provided by electoral bonds failed the test of proportionality because it completely tilted the balance toward privacy while abrogating the public’s informational interests, thereby failing to serve as the least restrictive means to protect those rights. This ruling is highly relevant to open data advocates as it establishes a legal precedent that transparency in political funding is a prerequisite for free and fair elections. It reinforces the principle that open data regarding financial flows to political entities is necessary to counteract political inequality and ensure accountability. Consequently, it supports the broader movement for accessible, public datasets that allow citizens to scrutinize the intersection of money and politics, ensuring that democratic processes remain responsive to the electorate rather than exclusive contributors.
