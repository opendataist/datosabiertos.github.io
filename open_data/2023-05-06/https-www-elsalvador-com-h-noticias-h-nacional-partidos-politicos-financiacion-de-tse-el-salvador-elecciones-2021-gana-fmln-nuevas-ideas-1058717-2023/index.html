El Salvador’s Supreme Electoral Tribunal has ordered three major political parties to provide details about their financing within a shortened timeframe, although its decision has sparked controversy by limiting access to supporting documents and to the specific destinations of expenditures. While some parties have begun submitting partial information, the ruling prevents citizens and oversight authorities from conducting a complete and transparent audit, which is seen as a lack of accountability in light of the legal commitment to transparency. The petitioning lawyers argue that the tribunal’s refusal to require the submission of data in a structured and systematized format, such as an editable file, violates the fundamental right of access to public information. This resistance to providing data in a format that is useful for analysis not only hinders the investigation of possible irregularities but also denies society effective tools to monitor how public resources and private donations are used in key electoral processes. This dispute is relevant to the field of open data because it illustrates the bureaucratic barriers that prevent genuine transparency in public management. The requirement for specific and accessible formats is crucial to enable the reuse and social scrutiny of data; without them, information remains inaccessible or difficult to process, undermining the possibility of an informed citizenry and democratic integrity.
