The Government's Antipathy Towards Transparency Has Made FOIA Lawsuits The Default Process

The Government's Antipathy Towards Transparency Has Made FOIA Lawsuits The Default Process

The article highlights a critical failure in government transparency, where agencies like the Department of Homeland Security routinely ignore Freedom of Information Act requests. Rather than proactively disclosing records regarding Border Patrol’s inland operations, these entities force citizens into litigation to access basic public information. This dynamic fundamentally undermines the intent of the law, transforming a mechanism for accountability into a barrier that requires legal intervention to overcome. This trend signifies that FOIA lawsuits have shifted from being a last resort to a standard procedural step. Law enforcement and counterterrorism agencies, in particular, exhibit high levels of non-compliance, stalling or ignoring requests to limit their exposure. By relying on the resource-intensive nature of legal battles, these agencies exploit the fact that most individuals lack the time or money to fight for transparency, thereby effectively hiding their activities from public scrutiny until compelled by a court order. This erosion of access is directly relevant to open data advocates because it demonstrates how opaque government operations prevent effective public oversight. When essential data about surveillance, policy, and enforcement remains locked behind legal hurdles, citizens cannot make informed decisions or hold officials accountable. The article argues that this subversion of the FOIA system represents a betrayal of democratic ideals, suggesting that true transparency requires agencies to respect the law voluntarily rather than treating litigation as a mere administrative hurdle.
